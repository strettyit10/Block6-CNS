LO 1Skin receptors and the route each signal takesWhich receptor feels what, how fast it adapts, which fiber carries it, and which pathway it joins.

By the end: you can name the five skin receptors and their modalities, predict slow vs fast adaptation, match Aβ/Aδ/C fibers to modalities, explain two-point discrimination and the homunculus, and say whether a signal goes up the DCML, the anterolateral system or the trigeminal route.
Q1. How do we classify sensory receptors in the first place?

By location

  • Exteroceptors → skin / body surface → touch, pressure, pain, temperature
  • Proprioceptors → muscles, tendons, joints → stretch, joint position, movement
  • Enteroceptors (visceroceptors) → internal organs → gut distension, blood pH, other internal changes

By stimulus

  • Mechanoreceptors → pressure, vibration, stretch (skin, ear, muscle)
  • Thermoreceptors → temperature (skin, hypothalamus)
  • Nociceptors → pain
  • Chemoreceptors → O₂, CO₂, pH (carotid and aortic bodies)
  • Photoreceptors → light (rods, cones)
CheckA muscle spindle in the quadriceps: classify it by location and by stimulus.
Location: proprioceptor (muscle). Stimulus: mechanoreceptor (it senses stretch).

Step 1 HY — receptors
  • Pacinian = vibration + deep pressure, lamellated, fast.
  • Meissner = light discriminatory touch in glabrous skin, fast.
  • Merkel = static pressure, texture, slow.
  • Ruffini = stretch, joint angle, slow.
  • Free nerve endings = pain/temp via Aδ and C.
Memory tool

"Pacinian = Pulsing (vibration), Meissner = Mild touch, Merkel = Maintained pressure, Ruffini = Rubber-band stretch." The two that end in -cian/-sner (corpuscles) adapt fast; the disc and the ending (Merkel, Ruffini) adapt slow.

Q3. What does "adaptation" mean, and how do Merkel cells differ from corpuscles at the molecular level?

Adaptation = how a receptor's firing changes during a constant stimulus.

Merkel cell → slow adapting

  • Pressure opens Piezo2 mechanosensitive channels in the Merkel cell
  • Merkel cell releases serotonin onto the Aβ terminal
  • Acts on ionotropic and metabotropic receptors → sustained firing → slow adapting

Meissner / Pacinian → fast adapting

  • Physical deformation opens pressure-sensitive Na⁺ channels in the axon itself
  • Na⁺ influx → generator potential → action potential
  • Capsule layers soak up steady pressure → fast adapting
  • Everyday example: you stop noticing the chair once seated (deep pressure adapts rapidly), but a pinprick keeps hurting (nociceptors = no adaptation).
  • Contrast to know: Merkel = serotonin release, ionotropic + metabotropic receptors, slow adapting vs Meissner/Pacinian = deformation opens pressure-sensitive Na⁺ channels, Na⁺ in → AP, fast adapting.
CheckWhich transmitter does a Merkel cell release, and what kind of adaptation results?
Serotonin, acting on ionotropic + metabotropic receptors on the nerve terminal → slow adaptation.
Step 1 trap

"Slow adapting" does not mean slow conduction. Merkel and Meissner both use the same fast Aβ fiber; adaptation is a property of the receptor ending, conduction speed is a property of the axon.

Q4. Which fiber carries which sensation, and how fast?

Rule: bigger + more myelin = faster. Touch and proprioception get the express lane; pain and temperature take the local.

  • Discriminative touch and pressure use fast, myelinated Aβ fibers.
CheckYou touch a hot pan. Which fiber gives the instant sharp "ouch," and which gives the lingering ache?
Aδ = fast, sharp first pain. C = slow, dull second pain (unmyelinated, ~1 m/s).
Step 1 HY — fibers

Local anesthetics block small, myelinated fibers first (Aδ pain), then C, then Aβ touch, then Aα motor/proprioception. Boards like "patient feels pressure but no pain after lidocaine."

Memory tool

A-β = "Big touch", A-δ = "Darn, that's hot!", C = "Crawling slow". Alphabet order = shrinking size = slowing speed: α > β > δ > C.

Q5. Why can your lips tell two points apart but your back cannot? (two-point discrimination and the homunculus)
  • Two-point discrimination = the smallest gap at which two touching points feel like two.
  • It depends on receptor density and receptive-field size:
    • Fingertips and lips: dense receptors, tiny fields → only a few mm (lips ~7 mm, fingertips ~2–3 mm)
    • Back, calf, thigh: sparse receptors, large fields → ~42 mm
  • The primary somatosensory cortex (S1, postcentral gyrus) is somatotopic: neighboring body parts map to neighboring cortex.
  • The sensory homunculus shows cortical area per body part, scaled to receptor density / acuity, not to body size → huge hands, lips, tongue.
CheckA test for two-point discrimination is abnormal on the right fingertips. Which ascending pathway is in trouble?
The DCML (right dorsal column below the medulla, or the left lemniscus / VPL / cortex above it). Two-point discrimination is a fine, discriminative touch test.
Step 1 HY — homunculus and strokes

ACA stroke → contralateral leg/foot sensory + motor loss (medial surface). MCA stroke → contralateral face and arm > leg. Loss of two-point discrimination, vibration and position = dorsal column problem.

Buzzwords
"onion-skin capsule" → Pacinian corpuscle "dermal papillae, glabrous skin" → Meissner corpuscle "Piezo2 / serotonin" → Merkel cell "no adaptation" → nociceptor "42 mm on the back" → poor two-point discrimination "medial surface leg" → ACA territory
Q6. Once inside the cord, which pathway does each signal take — and what replaces it for the face?

DCML

Touch, pressure, vibration, conscious proprioception (fine, discriminative)

Aβ + Aα · crosses in the medulla

Anterolateral (spinothalamic)

Pain and temperature (+ crude touch)

Aδ + C · crosses in the cord

Spinocerebellar

Non-conscious proprioception → cerebellum

Spindles, tendon organs

  • Body touch → DCML → VPL → cortex.
  • Face touch → CN V → principal (main) sensory trigeminal nucleus → VTTT/DTTT → VPM → cortex (the "DCML of the face").
  • The ascending pathways sit next to the descending corticospinal tract (motor) — "start thinking in 3D": one lesion often hits several tracts.
CheckVibration on the cheek vs vibration on the shin: which thalamic nucleus receives each?
Cheek → VPM (trigeminal route). Shin → VPL (DCML).
Step 1 trap

Crude touch travels with pain/temp in the anterior spinothalamic part of the anterolateral system. So a patient with dorsal column loss still feels a cotton swab, but cannot localize it or tell two points apart. Do not answer "total loss of touch" for a dorsal column lesion.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1Put on a wristwatch: you feel it for a few seconds and then forget it is there. Which receptors made that happen?
Rapidly adapting receptors (Meissner, Pacinian). They fire at application and removal, then go quiet with steady pressure.
A2A drug blocks Piezo2 in the skin. Which receptor loses most of its function, and what sensation suffers?
Merkel cell → impaired sustained pressure / texture / shape discrimination. Piezo2 opening triggers its serotonin release.
A3On nerve conduction testing, a patient's fastest sensory fibers conduct at 50 m/s. Which fiber class and modality is that?
Aβ (35–75 m/s) → touch, pressure, vibration from skin mechanoreceptors.
A4Why is the hand on the homunculus bigger than the entire trunk?
Cortical area scales with receptor density and acuity, not body size; the hand has dense, small-field receptors (fine two-point discrimination).

LO 2The DCML and the trigeminal touch pathwayCell bodies, first synapse, ascending tract, crossing point — then the lesions.

By the end: you can draw both 3-neuron relays from memory, say exactly where each crosses, and use that to predict whether a lesion gives ipsilateral or contralateral loss.
Q1. How do large and small fibers sort themselves out as the dorsal root enters the cord?
  • Axons enter through the dorsal root entry zone (DREZ), a wide zone spanning white and gray matter; sensory axons can enter anywhere in it, sorted by size:
    • Medial to the dorsal horn = myelinated (Aβ)
    • Lateral to the dorsal horn = lightly/unmyelinated (Aδ, C)
  • Medial division = large, myelinated (Aβ) → turn up into the dorsal column (DCML).
  • Lateral division = small, lightly or unmyelinated (Aδ, C) → Lissauer's tract / dorsal horn → anterolateral system.
CheckA surgeon cuts only the lateral part of a dorsal rootlet (DREZ lesioning for pain). What is spared?
The medial, large myelinated Aβ fibers → touch, vibration and position sense are spared; pain/temperature input is reduced.
Memory tool

"Big guys walk down the middle aisle." Large Aβ fibers go medial into the dorsal column; small fibers stay lateral.

Q2. What are fasciculus gracilis and cuneatus, and why are they arranged S-L-T-C?
  • The dorsal column on each side has two bundles, split by the posterior intermediate sulcus:
    • Fasciculus gracilis → medial → lower body (legs; below T6, i.e. T7 and down)
    • Fasciculus cuneatus → lateral → upper body (arms; T6 and above)
  • Axons line up in the order they arrive: each new level is added on the lateral side, pushing older fibers medially → medial to lateral = S → L → T → C.
  • Consequence: below T6 there is no cuneatus — a lumbar section shows only gracilis.
CheckOnly the lateral part of the right dorsal column is damaged at C4. What is lost?
Right fasciculus cuneatus → vibration, position and fine touch in the right arm / upper body; legs (gracilis, medial) are spared.
Step 1 HY

Boards phrase it as: gracilis = T7 and below (legs), cuneatus = T6 and above (arms). Same line, different wording.

Memory tool

"Graceful legs walk down the middle; the cuneiform arms reach out." Gracilis = legs = medial; cuneatus = arms = lateral. "Legs in, arms out."

The DCML is a 3-neuron relay that transmits touch, pressure and conscious proprioception. Body chain: DRG → fasciculus gracilis/cuneatus → synapse in nucleus gracilis/cuneatus (closed medulla) → decussate via internal arcuate fibers → medial lemniscus → synapse in VPL → sensory cortex.

Somatotopy is kept the whole way (S-L-T-C in the cord → leg/arm bands in the lemniscus → homunculus).

Conscious proprioception rides the same route: large myelinated fibers from muscle spindles (Ia, II) and Golgi tendon organs (Ib). The non-conscious copy of that data goes to the cerebellum (spinocerebellar tracts).

CheckName the structure where the DCML crosses and the tract the crossed fibers form.
Crosses as the internal arcuate fibers in the caudal (closed) medulla → forms the medial lemniscus.
Step 1 HY

DCML: up first, cross in the medulla. Spinothalamic: cross first (anterior white commissure, within 1–2 segments), then up. This one difference generates every Brown-Séquard question.

Step 1 trap

The DCML does not cross at the pyramidal decussation — that is the corticospinal (motor) tract. Sensory decussation (internal arcuate fibers) sits slightly rostral and dorsal to it in the caudal medulla.

VPM = "Makeup goes on the face (and in the Mouth)" → face + taste. VPL = Limbs. LGN = Light, MGN = Music.

A small VPL/VPM lacunar infarct → pure contralateral hemisensory loss (face + body). Weeks later: thalamic pain syndrome (Dejerine-Roussy) — burning contralateral pain and allodynia.

Q5. How does the face do the same job? (the principal sensory trigeminal pathway)
  • CN V is the main sensory nerve of the face: V1 ophthalmic (forehead, upper lid, cornea, nose bridge), V2 maxillary (cheek, upper lip, upper teeth, palate), V3 mandibular (lower lip, chin, jaw, lower teeth, general sensation of anterior tongue). It also runs the muscles of mastication.
  • CN V has several brainstem nuclei; sensory nuclei sit lateral to the sulcus limitans, motor nuclei medial to it.

In the ipsilateral principal (main) sensory nucleus of V, mid-pons. (Pain from the chin would instead synapse in the spinal trigeminal nucleus.)
Step 1 trap
  • Principal nucleus = touch (pons). Spinal nucleus = pain/temp (medulla, down to C2). A lateral medullary stroke spares facial touch but kills facial pain/temp.
  • Angle of the jaw is C2/C3 (great auricular), not V3.
Memory tool

Trigeminal exits: "Standing Room Only" → V1 Superior orbital fissure, V2 foramen Rotundum, V3 foramen Ovale. VTTT = Via the other side (crossed); DTTT = Doesn't cross.

Q6. A lesion is ipsilateral or contralateral — how do I decide every time?

One rule: before the crossing → ipsilateral deficit; after the crossing → contralateral deficit. In the cord, a lesion hits fibers entering at that level and all those coming up from below; higher segments have not entered yet.

Lesion siteSide of deficitRegion affected
Peripheral nerve / dorsal rootIpsilateralThat nerve's / dermatome's territory (all modalities)
Dorsal column (cord)IpsilateralDermatomes at and below the lesion
Nucleus gracilis / cuneatusIpsilateralLeg (gracilis) or arm (cuneatus)
Medial lemniscus (rostral medulla, pons, midbrain)Contralateral (already crossed)Whole body below the face
VPL / internal capsule / S1ContralateralBody (VPL) ± face (VPM); cortex follows homunculus
CN V or principal sensory nucleusIpsilateral faceFace touch
VTTT aloneLittle / no lossDTTT backup

Dorsal column damage — symptoms

  • Loss of tactile sense: vibration, deep touch, two-point discrimination, position sense; symptoms ipsilateral, in dermatomes at or below the lesion
  • Astereognosis: cannot describe an object in the hand with eyes closed
  • Sensory ataxic ("stomping") gait: slams the feet down to get feedback
  • Positive Romberg: steady with eyes open, sways/falls with eyes closed

Why Romberg works

  • Balance needs 2 of 3: proprioception, vision, vestibular
  • Dorsal column loss + eyes closed → only vestibular left → falls
  • Cerebellar ataxia is unsteady with eyes open and closed → Romberg separates sensory from cerebellar ataxia
CheckRight dorsal column destroyed at T8. Vibration lost in the right hand? In the right foot?
Hand: no (cuneatus fibers enter above T8). Foot: yes (gracilis fibers from below must pass T8).
Step 1 HY — Brown-Séquard (hemisection)

Right hemisection at T10: right loss of vibration/position below T10 (DCML), right UMN weakness below (corticospinal), left loss of pain/temp starting 1–2 levels below (spinothalamic already crossed), right LMN signs and all-modality loss at the level. Above T1 → ipsilateral Horner.

Step 1 trap

Romberg tests proprioception, not the cerebellum. A patient who falls only with eyes closed has a sensory ataxia (dorsal columns or large-fiber neuropathy); a cerebellar patient is wobbly regardless.

Q7. Which diseases target the dorsal columns? (Step 1 tie-ins)

Subacute combined degeneration B12

Dorsal columns + lateral corticospinal + spinocerebellar (demyelination; ↑ methylmalonic acid).

Bilateral vibration/position loss, positive Romberg, spastic paresis, Babinski, ± macrocytic anemia. Pernicious anemia, vegans, metformin, gastrectomy, N₂O abuse.

Tabes dorsalis syphilis

Tertiary neurosyphilis → degeneration of dorsal roots and dorsal columns.

Positive Romberg, stomping gait, lightning (shooting) pains, Argyll Robertson pupils (accommodate, don't react), absent DTRs, Charcot joints.

Friedreich ataxia AR · GAA

GAA repeat in frataxin (FXN, chr 9) → mitochondrial iron overload.

Teen with ataxia, dorsal column + spinocerebellar + corticospinal loss, pes cavus, kyphoscoliosis, diabetes; death from hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Copper deficiency

Mimics B12 SCD (myelopathy with dorsal column signs). Think after bariatric surgery or excess zinc intake.

Posterior spinal artery infarct

Rare. Ipsilateral vibration/position loss below the lesion; motor largely spared (see LO 4).

Step 1 HY — classic vignettes
  • Older adult, glossitis, macrocytosis, falls in the dark, Babinski → B12 / SCD.
  • Man with shooting leg pains, small irregular pupils that accommodate but don't react to light → tabes dorsalis.
  • 14-year-old, worsening gait, high arches, scoliosis, abnormal ECG → Friedreich ataxia.
Buzzwords
"falls with eyes closed" → positive Romberg, dorsal columns "stomping gait" → sensory ataxia "can't name coin in hand, eyes closed" → astereognosis "lightning pains + Argyll Robertson" → tabes dorsalis "↑ methylmalonic acid" → B12 deficiency (SCD) "GAA repeat, frataxin" → Friedreich ataxia "internal arcuate fibers" → DCML decussation
Step 1 trap

Folate deficiency gives the same megaloblastic anemia as B12 but no dorsal column disease and normal methylmalonic acid. Giving folate alone to a B12-deficient patient fixes the blood and lets the cord keep degenerating.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A lesion destroys the left nucleus cuneatus. What deficit, and which side?
Left (ipsilateral) arm/upper body: loss of fine touch, vibration, position sense. The 2nd-order neurons there have not crossed yet.
A2A small infarct hits the left medial lemniscus in the pons. Which side loses vibration and position sense?
Right body (contralateral): the lemniscus carries fibers that already crossed in the caudal medulla.
A3A small lesion cuts only the right VTTT in the upper pons. Does the patient lose touch on the left face?
Mostly no. Touch from the face also reaches VPM through the uncrossed DTTT, a redundant route.
A4A man with a left L1 dorsal column lesion: what is lost and where?
Vibration, fine touch and position sense on the left from L1 and below; the DCML has not crossed in the cord.
A5A strict vegan has bilateral vibration loss in the feet, a positive Romberg and up-going toes. Which tracts are damaged?
Dorsal columns (vibration, Romberg) + lateral corticospinal tracts (Babinski) → subacute combined degeneration from B12 deficiency.

Q1. What does a cervical cord cross-section look like, and where is the DCML?
  • DCML lives in the dorsal funiculus, between the dorsal horns: gracilis medial, cuneatus lateral, split by the posterior intermediate sulcus (only present at T6 and above).
  • Cervical cord: large, oval, lots of white matter (all ascending fibers have joined), big ventral horns (arm motor neurons), both fasciculi present.
  • Lumbar cord: rounder, more gray than white, gracilis only.
  • Know how to draw and label: the cervical profile, the dorsal columns (gracilis medial = lower body, cuneatus lateral = upper body), the posterior intermediate sulcus, and the S-L-T-C order (medial to lateral).
CheckWhy is there more white matter at C5 than at L3?
Ascending fibers accumulate as you go up (every new level adds axons), and descending tracts are fullest at the top before peeling off to their targets.
Step 1 HY

Boards show a cord section with one region shaded and ask the deficit. Dorsal = DCML (ipsilateral vibration/position), lateral = corticospinal (ipsilateral UMN) + spinocerebellar, anterolateral = spinothalamic (contralateral pain/temp), ventral horn = LMN.

CheckAfter a thoracoabdominal aneurysm repair, a man can't move his legs or feel pinprick below the umbilicus, but feels a tuning fork on his toes. Which artery?
Anterior spinal artery territory (via loss of the artery of Adamkiewicz) → anterior cord syndrome; dorsal columns spared.
Step 1 HY — anterior spinal artery syndrome

Sudden bilateral paralysis + loss of pain/temperature, with preserved vibration and proprioception. Classic triggers: aortic aneurysm repair/dissection, aortic cross-clamping, hypotension. Watershed: upper-mid thoracic cord.

Step 1 trap

"Preserved vibration" points away from the dorsal columns. Don't pick B12 deficiency or tabes when the tuning fork is still felt; pick anterior spinal artery / anterior cord syndrome.

Q2. How is the brainstem supplied? (medial vs lateral, level by level)

General pattern: paramedian branches → medial structures (motor tracts, medial lemniscus, motor CN nuclei); circumferential branches → lateral structures (spinothalamic, spinal V, cerebellar peduncles, sympathetics).

  • Territory map: rostral medulla = posterior spinal, PICA, vertebral, anterior spinal; caudal medulla = posterior spinal, vertebral, anterior spinal.
  • For each territory, predict what is lost and on which side:
    • Ascending sensory (medial lemniscus, spinothalamic)
    • Descending motor (pyramid)
CheckWhich artery feeds the nucleus gracilis and cuneatus in the closed medulla?
The posterior spinal artery (dorsal territory of the caudal medulla).
Memory tool — "rule of 4s" (handy shortcut)
  • 4 medial structures start with M: Motor pathway (CST), Medial lemniscus, MLF, Motor nucleus of CN (III, IV, VI, XII).
  • 4 lateral structures start with S: Spinocerebellar, Spinothalamic, Sensory nucleus of V, Sympathetic pathway.
  • Medulla holds CN IX–XII, pons V–VIII, midbrain III–IV.
Q3. What are the classic brainstem stroke syndromes, and which of them touch the DCML?

Medial medullary ASA

Pyramid → contralateral hemiparesis. ML → contralateral vibration/position loss. CN XII → tongue deviates toward lesion.

DCML hit ✔

Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) PICA

Ipsilateral face pain/temp loss + contralateral body pain/temp loss, dysphagia, hoarseness (n. ambiguus), ipsi Horner, vertigo/nystagmus, ipsi limb ataxia.

DCML spared ✘ (ML is medial)

Medial pontine basilar paramedian

Corticospinal → contralateral hemiparesis; ML → contralateral vibration/position loss; ± ipsilateral CN VI (eye can't abduct).

Bilateral basilar → locked-in syndrome.

Lateral pontine AICA

Like Wallenberg plus facial paralysis (CN VII nucleus), hearing loss (VIII), ↓ lacrimation/salivation.

DCML spared.

Weber (medial midbrain) PCA paramedian

Ipsilateral CN III palsy (down and out, ptosis, dilated pupil) + contralateral hemiparesis (cerebral peduncle).

CheckLeft ASA occlusion in the rostral medulla: which way does the tongue point, and which side is weak?
Tongue → left (toward the lesion, left XII). Weakness and vibration/position loss → right body.
Step 1 HY — medial vs lateral

Medial brainstem stroke = motor + medial lemniscus + medial CN motor nucleus (crossed hemiparesis, "alternating" syndrome). Lateral = pain/temp crossed pattern + Horner + cerebellar + the level's CN, with power and vibration preserved.

Memory tool

"Don't PICK a horse (hoarse) that can't eat (dysphagia)" — nucleus ambiguus signs = PICA. "Facial droop means AICA's pooped." Medial medullary: "lick your wounds" — tongue points toward the lesion.

Step 1 trap

Wallenberg gives sensory loss but the DCML is spared — vibration and position remain normal on both sides. If the vignette has contralateral vibration loss + hemiparesis, it is medial, not lateral.

Buzzwords
"after aortic aneurysm repair" → ASA / Adamkiewicz "tongue deviates toward lesion + contralateral hemiparesis" → medial medullary "hoarseness, dysphagia, Horner" → PICA "ipsi facial paralysis + hearing loss" → AICA "down and out eye + contralateral weakness" → Weber "only blinks and looks up" → locked-in (basilar)

LO 5Taste: tongue to cortexThree cranial nerves, one nucleus, one thalamic relay.

By the end: you can name the nerve and ganglion for each taste region, explain the layout of the solitary nucleus, and trace taste through VPM to the gustatory cortex.
Q1. Which nerve carries taste from which part of the mouth?
NerveTaste region1st-order cell body (ganglion)
Facial (VII) — chorda tympaniAnterior 2/3 of tongueGeniculate
Glossopharyngeal (IX)Posterior 1/3 of tongueInferior (petrosal)
Vagus (X)Minor role: soft palate / epiglottis regionInferior (nodose)
  • Summary: VII → anterior 2/3; IX → posterior 1/3; X → minor role, any taste from soft palate and epiglottis. All gustatory fibers go to the solitary nucleus.
  • Taste is carried by special visceral afferents; general sensation (touch, pain) of the anterior 2/3 is V3 (lingual nerve), of the posterior 1/3 is IX.
  • Taste buds sit on papillae (fungiform anteriorly, circumvallate at the back); all five tastes are detected across the whole tongue.
CheckAfter middle-ear surgery, a patient loses taste on the front of the right tongue but still feels touch there. Which nerve?
The right chorda tympani (CN VII), which crosses the middle ear. Touch is preserved because it rides in the lingual nerve (V3).
Step 1 trap
  • Taste ≠ general sensation: anterior 2/3 taste = VII, touch/pain = V3. Posterior 1/3 = IX for both. Motor to the tongue = XII (palatoglossus = X).
  • Boards usually credit X with the epiglottis/pharynx; palate taste is mostly VII (greater petrosal). Pick X when the stem says epiglottis.
  • The old "tongue map" (sweet at the tip, bitter at the back) is a myth.
Q2. What is the solitary nucleus, and where does taste go from there?
  • The nucleus of the solitary tract (solitary nucleus) is a column in the dorsal medulla, wrapped around the solitary tract — the brainstem's visceral sensory nucleus.
  • Organized rostral → caudal:
    • Rostral → taste (gustatory nucleus)
    • Middle (medial) → respiratory and baroreceptor input
    • Caudal → visceral (gut, cardiorespiratory) sensation
  • Taste chain: taste receptors → CN VII/IX/X → synapse in solitary nucleus → VPM → sensory cortex (the tract from solitary nucleus to thalamus is not named).
  • From the rostral solitary nucleus → VPM of the thalamus (mainly ipsilateral; sometimes written "VP nucleus", the specific relay is VPM) → gustatory cortex (insula / frontal operculum, next to the face area of S1).
CheckWhich part of the solitary nucleus is the "gustatory nucleus," and which thalamic nucleus does it project to?
The rostral part; it projects to VPM.
Step 1 HY

Nucleus solitarius = visceral sensory: taste, baroreceptors (IX carotid sinus, X aortic arch), gut distension. Nucleus ambiguus = motor to pharynx/larynx (IX, X). Dorsal motor nucleus of X = parasympathetic to the viscera. VPM handles face sensation and taste.

Memory tool

"Solitary = Sensory; Ambiguus = Action (motor)." Taste is the "head" of the solitary nucleus (rostral); the gut is the "tail" (caudal).

Buzzwords
"chorda tympani / middle ear" → taste anterior 2/3 (VII) "geniculate ganglion" → VII sensory cell bodies "gustatory nucleus" → rostral solitary nucleus "insula / frontal operculum" → gustatory cortex

A1A Bell's palsy patient (lesion in the facial canal above the chorda tympani branch) reports food tastes "metallic and dull" on one side. Which region of the tongue is affected, and is general sensation lost?
Ipsilateral anterior 2/3 taste lost; general sensation preserved (V3 lingual nerve).

1A 68-year-old woman has 6 months of tingling feet and unsteadiness that is worse in the dark. Vibration and joint-position sense are absent at the toes bilaterally. She stands steadily with eyes open but sways and nearly falls with eyes closed. Knee reflexes are brisk and plantar responses are extensor. Hemoglobin is 9.8 g/dL with MCV 112 fL. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

B — Subacute combined degeneration. Dorsal columns (vibration/position loss, positive Romberg) + lateral corticospinal tracts (brisk reflexes, Babinski) + macrocytic anemia. Tabes (A) hits dorsal roots/columns but gives absent reflexes and no UMN signs. ASA infarct (C) spares vibration. Syringomyelia (D) gives cape-like pain/temp loss. ALS (E) has no sensory loss.

2A 60-year-old man has sudden loss of vibration and position sense in the right arm and right leg. Pain and temperature are normal. Strength is normal, and the face is unaffected. Where is the single lesion most likely located?

C. Arm + leg on the right with only DCML modalities = a lesion after the crossing, on the left: the left medial lemniscus. A right gracilis lesion (A) would spare the arm; right n. cuneatus (B) gives arm only; a left dorsal column (D) gives left-sided loss; VPM (E) relays the face, not the body.

3A researcher records from a cutaneous receptor in the deep dermis of the palm. The receptor responds briskly to a 250-Hz vibration and at the onset and offset of steady pressure, but is silent while the pressure is held. Histology shows concentric lamellae around a single nerve ending. Which receptor is this?

D — Pacinian corpuscle: deep, onion-skin capsule, high-frequency vibration, fast adapting. Merkel (A) and Ruffini (C) are slow adapting. Meissner (B) is fast but superficial (dermal papillae) and tuned to low-frequency flutter. Free nerve endings (E) have no capsule and carry pain/temp.

4Nerve conduction studies of the sural nerve show a sensory conduction velocity of 48 m/s in a healthy volunteer. The fibers generating this response most likely carry which information, by which axon class?

A. Aβ fibers (6–12 µm, heavily myelinated) conduct at ~35–75 m/s and carry cutaneous mechanoreception. Aα (B) are faster (80–120 m/s) and come from muscle, not a cutaneous nerve. Aδ (C) 5–30 m/s, C (D) 0.5–2 m/s. B fibers (E) are autonomic preganglionic, not sensory.

5A 25-year-old man is stabbed in the back. He has weakness of the right leg with a right Babinski sign. Which additional sensory findings are expected if the lesion is a right hemisection of the cord at T10?

E — Brown-Séquard. DCML has not crossed in the cord → ipsilateral vibration/position loss below the lesion. Spinothalamic fibers crossed within 1–2 segments → contralateral pain/temp loss starting 1–2 levels below. B and C reverse the sides. A fits a posterior-column lesion, D fits syringomyelia.

6A 72-year-old man undergoes open repair of a thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm. On waking he cannot move either leg and has lost pinprick and temperature sensation below the umbilicus bilaterally. Which modality is most likely preserved in his legs?

A. Anterior spinal artery infarct (artery of Adamkiewicz) destroys the anterior 2/3 of the cord: corticospinal (D), spinothalamic (B, C), autonomic pathways (E). The dorsal columns, supplied by the posterior spinal arteries, survive → vibration and proprioception are preserved.

7A 66-year-old woman suddenly develops right arm and leg weakness and loss of vibration and position sense on the right side. On protrusion, her tongue deviates to the left. Facial sensation and pain/temperature sensation are normal. Occlusion of which vessel best explains this?

B — Medial medullary syndrome. Left pyramid → right hemiparesis; left medial lemniscus → right DCML loss; left XII → tongue deviates left. PICA (A) and AICA (D) are lateral and spare motor/ML. A right-sided lesion (C) would deviate the tongue right. Basilar paramedian (E) is pontine and would involve CN VI, not XII.

8Second-order neurons in the principal sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve send most of their axons across the midline in the ventral trigeminothalamic tract. In which thalamic nucleus do these axons terminate?

E — VPM relays face sensation (and taste). VPL (A) relays the body (medial lemniscus, spinothalamic). LGN (B) vision, MGN (C) hearing, VA (D) motor loop from basal ganglia.

9After surgery for chronic otitis media on the left, a 40-year-old woman notes that food tastes bland on the front of the left side of her tongue. Light touch and pinprick on the same area are normal, and facial movements are symmetric. Which structure was most likely injured?

D. The chorda tympani (VII) crosses the middle ear and carries taste from the anterior 2/3. Lingual nerve (A) injury would also remove touch/pinprick. IX (B) serves the posterior 1/3. XII (C) is motor. Greater petrosal (E) carries palate taste and lacrimal secretomotor fibers, not anterior tongue.

10The central processes of taste fibers from the posterior third of the tongue make their first synapse in which structure?

C. All taste (VII, IX, X) synapses in the rostral (gustatory) solitary nucleus. Nucleus ambiguus (A) and dorsal motor nucleus of X (D) are motor. Spinal V (B) = face/oral pain-temp; principal V (E) = face touch.

11A 55-year-old man has episodes of sudden stabbing leg pain, a wide-based stomping gait and a positive Romberg sign. Knee and ankle reflexes are absent. His pupils are small and irregular; they constrict on near focus but not to light. Degeneration of which structures explains his gait?

D — Tabes dorsalis (tertiary syphilis) with Argyll Robertson pupils. Dorsal root/column degeneration → proprioceptive (sensory) ataxia, positive Romberg, areflexia, lightning pains. Anterior horn (A) → LMN weakness; corticospinal (B) → UMN signs; spinothalamic (C) → pain/temp loss; vermis (E) → truncal ataxia with eyes open and a negative Romberg.

12A 14-year-old girl has 2 years of progressive clumsiness and falls. Exam shows absent ankle reflexes, loss of vibration sense in the feet, bilateral Babinski signs, high-arched feet and kyphoscoliosis. ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy. Which genetic mechanism is most likely?

E — Friedreich ataxia (autosomal recessive, GAA in FXN): dorsal columns + spinocerebellar + corticospinal degeneration, pes cavus, scoliosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (leading cause of death). A = Huntington, B = myotonic dystrophy, C = fragile X, D = spinal muscular atrophy (pure LMN).

13A 70-year-old man has sudden numbness and weakness of the left foot and leg. His left arm and face are normal. Two-point discrimination on the left sole is impaired. Infarction in the territory of which artery best explains this?

A. The leg and foot are represented on the medial surface of the postcentral (and precentral) gyrus — ACA territory — and cortex controls the contralateral body. MCA (B) → face/arm > leg. Left ACA (C) → right leg. PCA (D) → vision/thalamus. A left posterior spinal infarct (E) would cause sensory loss without weakness.
