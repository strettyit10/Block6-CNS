Spinal Cord & Brainstem Anatomy

  6. LO 6Explain how the plates of the developing neural tube create the layout of the spinal cord and brainstem.

Q1. What wraps the spinal cord inside the bony canal?

The cord wears the same three meninges as the brain, from outside in:

Quick vocabulary you will use all lecture: sulcus = shallow groove · fissure = deep groove · commissure = a bridge where fibers cross to the other side.

CheckWhich meningeal layer makes the denticulate ligaments, and which layer makes the root sleeve?
Pia → denticulate ligaments (and filum terminale). Dura → dural root sleeve, which blends into the spinal nerve's epineurium.
Step 1 trap

Epidural ≠ spinal anesthesia. An epidural stops in the fat outside the dura; a spinal (and an LP) goes through dura and arachnoid into the subarachnoid space. The pia is never pierced.

Q2. Where does the cord end, and what is below it?

Top to bottom

  • Conus medullaris = tapered end of the cord, at L1–L2 in adults
  • Cauda equina ("horse's tail") = lumbar, sacral and coccygeal roots drifting down the canal to reach their own foramina
  • Lumbar cistern = CSF-filled subarachnoid space from the conus to S2, where the dural sac ends
  • Filum terminale (pia): internum inside the sac → externum (coccygeal ligament) anchors to the coccyx
  • Dorsal root ganglia sit in the intervertebral foramina; dorsal root = sensory, ventral root = motor; they join into a mixed spinal nerve
CheckWhy do lumbar and sacral roots have to run so far down inside the canal?
Each root must still leave through its own intervertebral foramen. The cord ends high (L1–L2), so the lower roots travel a long way down before they can exit → the cauda equina.
Q3. Why does the cord end higher as we grow?
Early fetusCord fills the whole canal; roots exit straight out
→
NewbornCord tip ≈ L3
→
AdultCord tip ≈ L1–L2
  • The vertebral column grows faster and longer than the cord, so the cord tip appears to "rise".
  • The roots stay attached to their own foramina → they become longer and more slanted the lower you go.
  • Practical result: in an infant the cord sits lower, so the needle goes one space lower (L4–L5).
CheckPredict: a 6-week-old needs an LP. Is L2–L3 safe?
No. A newborn's cord can reach about L3, so L2–L3 risks hitting the cord. Use L4–L5.
Q4. How do I choose the level for a spinal tap, and what does the needle pass through?
  • Adult: L3–L4 (L4–L5 also acceptable). Child: L4–L5 → a child's cord and column are closer in length, so the cord ends lower.
  • Know the names again: cord ends near L2 → end = conus medullaris; tether = filum terminale; roots = cauda equina.
  • Landmark: a line joining the tops of the iliac crests crosses the L4 spinous process (supracristal line).
  • Why it's safe: below the conus only mobile roots float in CSF; they drift away from the needle.

Q5. Conus medullaris vs cauda equina: what changes when the lesion moves a few centimeters?

The conus is cord (CNS, can give UMN signs); the cauda equina is roots (peripheral = LMN).

CheckA large central L4–L5 disc compresses the roots in the canal. UMN or LMN picture?
LMN. The cauda equina is peripheral nerve roots → flaccid, areflexic weakness, saddle anesthesia, urinary retention.
Step 1 HY — cauda equina

Saddle anesthesia + urinary retention/overflow + ↓ anal tone + bilateral sciatica = cauda equina syndrome → emergent MRI and surgical decompression. Common causes: large central disc herniation, tumor, epidural abscess or hematoma.

Step 1 trap

A lesion below L2 vertebra cannot give a Babinski sign or spasticity: there is no cord there. UMN signs point to the conus or higher.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A resident wants to do an LP on a 45-year-old at the L1–L2 interspace "to get more CSF." What is the risk?
Puncturing the conus medullaris. The adult cord ends at L1–L2; go to L3–L4 or L4–L5, where only cauda equina roots float in the lumbar cistern.
A2Why is the dural root sleeve clinically relevant when a dye is injected into the subarachnoid space (myelogram)?
Dye outlines each root sleeve into the foramen. The sleeve carries the subarachnoid space a short way along the root before the dura blends with the epineurium, so a compressed root shows as a "cut-off" sleeve.
A3A 62-year-old has 2 weeks of worsening asymmetric leg pain, flaccid weakness of both legs, absent ankle reflexes and numbness over the buttocks and perineum. No Babinski sign. Localize.
Cauda equina. Gradual, asymmetric, radicular, pure LMN with saddle anesthesia = roots below the conus.
A4A 3-month fetus and an adult each have a needle placed at L3. Which one is in danger of hitting cord?
The fetus. Early in development the cord fills the whole canal; only later does the column outgrow it.

LO 2Spinal cord cross-sections, level by levelDraw it, label it, and name the level of an unlabeled section

By the end: you can draw a cord section with its sulci, horns, funiculi and main tracts, and name a section as cervical, thoracic, lumbar or sacral from three clues: white-matter amount, ventral-horn size, lateral horn.
Q1. What are the landmarks on the outside of a cord section?
  • Posterior (dorsal) median sulcus — shallow midline groove at the back.
  • Posterior intermediate sulcus — splits each dorsal column into gracilis (medial) and cuneatus (lateral). Present only above T6.
  • Posterolateral sulcus — where dorsal rootlets enter (dorsal root entry zone).
  • Anterior (ventral) median fissure — deep front groove (the anterior spinal artery lies here).
  • Anterior white commissure — the bridge in front of the central canal where fibers cross (pain/temperature 2nd-order axons).
CheckYou see two dorsal columns on each side, separated by a little groove. What does that tell you about the level?
The posterior intermediate sulcus is present → you are at or above T6 (cuneatus only exists where upper-limb fibers have joined).
Step 1 trap — staining

On a myelin stain the "white" matter is dark and the "gray" matter is pale. On a Nissl stain it flips (cell bodies dark). Read the image before you read the shape.

Memory tool

Fissure = front (deep, like a fault line); sulcus = back (shallow). Both start with the letter that matches their size: Fissure is Fat and deep.

Q2. What is inside: horns, funiculi and the main tracts?

Dorsal horn — sensory

  • Receives dorsal-root input
  • Substantia gelatinosa (lamina II) and nucleus proprius: pain/temp relay
  • Built from the alar plate

Lateral horn — visceral

  • Intermediolateral cell column: sympathetic preganglionics, T1–L2/3
  • Sacral S2–S4: parasympathetic preganglionics in lateral gray

Ventral horn — motor

  • α and γ lower motor neurons
  • Medial = axial, lateral = limbs; flexors dorsal, extensors ventral
  • Built from the basal plate

White matter = three funiculi

  • Dorsal, lateral and ventral funiculi (columns), each holding parallel bundles of myelinated and unmyelinated axons.
  • Axons both ascend and descend; white matter also holds root fibers entering/leaving, glia and blood vessels.
  • Lissauer's tract (= dorsolateral funiculus/fasciculus) caps the dorsal horn: small pain/temp fibers run up or down 1–2 segments here before synapsing.

Tracts to find on a section

  • Dorsal funiculus (DCML) → gracilis carries information from below T6; cuneatus from above T6; the posterior intermediate sulcus separates them.
  • Anterolateral system runs anterolaterally → 2nd-order fibers cross in the anterior white commissure.
  • Clarke's nucleus (dorsal nucleus, medial base of the dorsal horn) exists only C8 to L2 → feeds the dorsal spinocerebellar tract
    • Leg information below L2 rides up in the gracilis until it reaches Clarke's
  • Corticospinal: UMN starts in layer 5 of motor cortex → LMNs = α and γ motor neurons in the ventral horn.
CheckWhich horn would you expect to be absent in a C6 section, and why?
The lateral horn. Sympathetic preganglionic cells live only from T1 to L2/3; C6 has no intermediolateral column.

Q4. How do cervical, thoracic, lumbar and sacral sections differ?

Two simple rules explain everything:

  • White matter shrinks as you go down. High levels carry ascending axons from every segment below plus descending axons to every segment below.
  • Ventral horns bulge where limbs are. More muscle → more LMNs → cervical (C5–T1) and lumbar (L2–S2) enlargements.

  • White matter amount reduces caudally → sacral is a small segment with proportionally more gray than white.
CheckAn unknown section is round with big ventral horns and a single dorsal column on each side. Cervical or lumbar?
Lumbar. Big ventral horns mean an enlargement; one dorsal column (no cuneatus) and less white matter mean below T6 → lumbar enlargement.
Memory tool

"Cervical is Chunky, Thoracic is Thin, Lumbar is Loaded (horns), Sacral is Small." A lateral horn = the "T" level has a Tab sticking out sideways.

Step 1 trap

Cervical and lumbar both have big ventral horns. Break the tie with white matter (cervical ≫ lumbar) and shape (cervical is a wide oval; lumbar is round) and the cuneatus (cervical only).

Q6. Who feeds the cord, and what does a vascular lesion look like?

One anterior spinal artery (ASA)

  • Runs in the anterior median fissure; from the vertebral arteries, reinforced by segmental arteries incl. the artery of Adamkiewicz (≈ T9–L2, usually left)
  • Supplies the anterior 2/3: ventral horns, corticospinal, anterolateral system
  • Single midline vessel → occlusion gives bilateral deficits

Two posterior spinal arteries (PSA)

  • One per side, near the dorsal root entry
  • Supply the posterior 1/3: dorsal columns
  • One-sided occlusion → ipsilateral dorsal-column loss
  • Loss of the posterior spinal arteries → loss of DCML (PSA problem → ipsilateral).
  • Loss of the anterior spinal artery → loss of ALS and corticospinal (ASA problem → bilateral).
Step 1 HY — anterior spinal artery syndrome

Classic setting: aortic aneurysm repair or dissection (Adamkiewicz compromised). Picture: bilateral spastic paralysis below the lesion (flaccid at the level), bilateral loss of pain/temp, bladder dysfunction, with vibration and proprioception spared (dorsal columns live on the PSAs).

Q7. How do cord sections read a lesion? (and the opening cases)

After a cord lesion, cut axons degenerate away from their cell bodies and lose their myelin (pale on a myelin stain):

Sensory (ascending) tracts

Cell bodies are below → tract looks pale ABOVE the lesion, normal below. E.g., T9 lesion: gracilis pale at C5, cuneatus normal (arm fibers entered above T9).

Motor (descending) tracts

Cell bodies are in the cortex → corticospinal looks pale BELOW the lesion, normal above. Clinically: spastic paralysis, positive Babinski below.

Spinocerebellar fibers entering below the lesion are also lost (seen above L2, since Clarke's nucleus only starts there).

Brown-Séquard (hemisection) ladder fall

Same side, below lesion: UMN weakness + hyperreflexia (lateral corticospinal), loss of fine touch/vibration/proprioception (dorsal column).

Opposite side, 1–2 segments below: loss of pain + temperature (spinothalamic already crossed).

At the level: ipsilateral LMN signs + band of total sensory loss.

Syringomyelia burnt hands

Fluid cavity (syrinx) expands the central canal, usually C8–T1 → first destroys crossing fibers in the anterior white commissure.

Bilateral "cape-like" loss of pain + temperature in arms/shoulders, touch spared. Enlarging → ventral horns (LMN atrophy of hands), lateral horn (Horner).

Associated with Chiari I malformation and trauma.

CheckLadder-fall case: the LEFT leg is paralyzed, hyperreflexic and can't feel a Q-tip; the RIGHT leg can't feel pinprick or heat. Which half of the cord is cut?
Left hemisection (thoracic, since the arms are fine). Motor and dorsal-column losses are on the lesion side; pain/temp loss is on the opposite side.
Step 1 HY — classic cord syndromes
  • Brown-Séquard: ipsilateral UMN + DCML loss, contralateral pain/temp loss (stab wound, trauma).
  • Syringomyelia: cape-like bilateral pain/temp loss; Chiari I.
  • Tabes dorsalis (tertiary syphilis): dorsal columns + roots → sensory ataxia, positive Romberg, Argyll Robertson pupils.
  • Subacute combined degeneration (B12, copper, nitrous oxide): dorsal columns + lateral corticospinal + spinocerebellar.
  • ALS: combined UMN + LMN, no sensory loss.
  • Polio / SMA: ventral horn only → LMN signs.
Step 1 trap

In Brown-Séquard, pain/temp loss starts 1–2 segments below the lesion on the opposite side (fibers ride Lissauer's tract before crossing). Don't put the pain/temp loss on the same side as the weakness.

Buzzwords
"cape-like"/"shawl" loss of pain and temp → syringomyelia"painless hand injuries from heat" → syrinx"stab wound to the back" → Brown-Séquard"after aortic aneurysm repair" → anterior spinal artery

A1A myelin-stained section has thin horns, a small pointed lateral horn, a rounded cluster of large cells at the medial base of the dorsal horn, and only one dorsal column per side. Level?
Lower thoracic (T7–T12). Lateral horn + Clarke's nucleus = thoracic; no cuneatus = below T6.

Diaphragm irritation refers to the shoulder (C3–C5); early appendicitis to the umbilicus (T10); cardiac pain to the left arm (T1–T4). Use dermatomes, not the organ's location.

LO 4Motor neurons, motor units and the Renshaw cellThe cells of the gray matter, and how tetanus hijacks one of them

By the end: you can define UMN vs LMN, α vs γ motor neuron, motor unit vs motor pool, explain small vs large units, and draw the Renshaw feedback loop and where tetanus toxin breaks it.
Q1. What kinds of neurons live in the spinal gray?

Visceral motor neurons

Sympathetic preganglionics: T1–L2/3 intermediolateral cell column → sympathetic chain or (splanchnics) prevertebral ganglia → glands, cardiac and smooth muscle.

Parasympathetic preganglionics: S2–S4 → long fibers to ganglia near/in pelvic organs.

Somatic motor neurons (LMNs)

α and γ motor neurons in the ventral horn, grouped in topographic columns.

Interneurons

The majority of gray-matter neurons; fully contained in the cord; link segments; relay sensory → motor; excitatory or inhibitory.

Sensory processing neurons

Dorsal-horn projection neurons that start ascending pathways (next lectures).

Memory tool

α = Action (the muscle you see moving). γ = Gain (tunes the spindle's sensitivity). Flexors are "up" (dorsal), extensors "down" (ventral); limbs go out to the side (lateral).

Q4. Motor unit vs motor pool: why do size and spread matter?

Motor unit

One α motor neuron + all the muscle fibers it innervates.

  • Small units (≈ 1:10) → fine control: extraocular, finger muscles
  • Large units (≈ 1:1000) → high force: gastrocnemius, postural

Motor pool

All the motor neurons that innervate one muscle.

  • Forms a longitudinal column that can span several segments
  • Biceps: musculocutaneous n., ≈ C5–C7 → losing one root = weakness, not paralysis
CheckWhy is a single root lesion rarely paralyzing for a limb muscle?
The muscle's motor pool spans several segments; one root removes only part of the pool → weakness.
Step 1 HY — size principle

Henneman size principle: small motor units are recruited first, large ones later as force rises. EMG in denervation shows fibrillations; reinnervation produces giant motor unit potentials.

Step 1 trap

Motor unit = one neuron (many fibers). Motor pool = many neurons (one muscle). Don't swap them in a "which term" stem.

Q5. What is the Renshaw cell, and how does tetanus break it?
  • Renshaw cell = an inhibitory (glycinergic) interneuron in the ventral horn.
  • Input: an excitatory collateral of the α motor neuron. Output: glycine back onto that same α motor neuron (and its neighbors).
  • Job: a feedback brake that prevents continuous firing.
  • Targets of Clostridium tetani toxin and of strychnine.
    • Context: tetanus vaccine developed 1924; about 1 million deaths/year in 1980 → about 20,000 today
AgentMechanismResult
Tetanospasmin (C. tetani)Retrograde axonal transport to cord → protease cleaves synaptobrevin (SNARE) in inhibitory interneurons → blocks glycine & GABA releaseSpastic paralysis: trismus (lockjaw), risus sardonicus, opisthotonos
StrychnineGlycine-receptor antagonistSimilar spasms, convulsions
Botulinum toxin (contrast)Cleaves SNAREs at the NMJ → blocks ACh releaseFlaccid paralysis, descending
CheckBoth tetanus and botulinum toxins cleave SNARE proteins. Why opposite paralysis types?
Different target cells. Tetanus silences inhibitory interneurons (Renshaw) → too much motor output (spastic). Botulinum silences the NMJ → no ACh (flaccid).
Step 1 HY — tetanus

Deep, dirty puncture wound + incomplete vaccination → trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonos, autonomic instability. Treat: tetanus immune globulin + tetanus toxoid vaccine, wound debridement, metronidazole, benzodiazepines (boost GABA-A) for spasms. Neonatal tetanus from unclean cord care.

Step 1 trap

Tetanus toxin does not act at the NMJ, and it does not destroy motor neurons. It blocks release of inhibitory transmitters (glycine, GABA) in the CNS.

Buzzwords
"lockjaw" / "fixed grin" / "arched back" → tetanus"rusty nail, no booster" → C. tetani"rat poison with convulsions" → strychnine, glycine receptor"recurrent inhibition" → Renshaw cell

Q3. How do I localize a brainstem lesion? (rule of 4s, intro)

4 Medial "M"s

  • Motor pathway (corticospinal) → contralateral weakness
  • Medial lemniscus → contralateral touch/vibration loss
  • MLF → internuclear ophthalmoplegia
  • Motor nuclei: III, IV, VI, XII (the ones that divide evenly into 12) → ipsilateral

4 Side "S"s

  • Spinocerebellar → ipsilateral ataxia
  • Spinothalamic → contralateral body pain/temp
  • Sensory nucleus of V → ipsilateral face pain/temp
  • Sympathetic pathway → ipsilateral Horner

4 levels × 4 nerves: above pons I–IV (midbrain III, IV) · pons V–VIII · medulla IX–XII. A crossed finding (ipsilateral cranial nerve + contralateral body) = brainstem.

Step 1 HY — rule of 4s

LO 6The neural tube blueprintWhy sensory is dorsal in the cord and lateral in the brainstem

By the end: you can name the alar and basal plates and the sulcus limitans, trace each into the adult cord, explain the "open book" shift in the brainstem, and place the seven functional columns from medial to lateral.
Q1. How does the neural tube become the spinal cord?
  • The neural tube wall thickens into a dorsal alar plate and a ventral basal plate, separated by a groove in the lumen: the sulcus limitans.
  • Alar → dorsal horn (sensory processing). Basal → ventral horn (motor).
  • The intermediate zone between them → lateral horn (visceral motor).
  • Dorsal root ganglia come from neural crest, not from the tube. Somites (muscles the LMNs drive) come from paraxial mesoderm.
  • Motor = medial, from the basal plate (GSE, GVE, SVE). Sensory = lateral, from the alar plate (GVA, SVA, GSA, SSA); the sulcus limitans separates them.
CheckWhich germ-line structure makes the dorsal root ganglion cells: alar plate or neural crest?
Neural crest. The alar plate makes the dorsal horn (inside the cord), not the ganglion outside it.
Step 1 trap

"Alar" sounds like "alarm" → a sensory alarm. Don't flip it: the basal plate is the base of movement. And remember: dorsal in the cord = lateral in the brainstem.
