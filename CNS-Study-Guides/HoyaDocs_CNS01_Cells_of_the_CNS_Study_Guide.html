Q1. What cell types are in the CNS, and how do gray and white matter differ?
  • Two big families: neurons and glia (non-neuronal cells). The three differentiated glia of the mature CNS are astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and microglia.
  • Other cells you will meet: specialized (non-fenestrated) capillary endothelium + its basement ("basilar") membrane, and ependymal cells lining the ventricles.

Q2. What makes a cell a "glial" cell, and how do the three types look?
  • Non-neuronal: they do not fire action potentials.
  • Have complex processes extending from the cell body (for contact and communication).
  • Can divide and give rise to new glia (all three types proliferate) → contrast: mature neurons do not divide.
  • Jobs: help define synaptic contacts, maintain neurons' signaling ability, maintain CNS homeostasis.
CheckWhy do primary brain tumors arise from glia rather than from mature neurons?
Glia keep the ability to divide; mature neurons are post-mitotic. Disordered glial division → gliomas.
Step 1 trap

Glia are not electrically silent bystanders: astrocytes have neurotransmitter receptors and respond with Ca²⁺ signals, but they still never fire action potentials. "Generates an action potential" always points to a neuron.

Q3. What are astrocytes and what do they do?

Astrocyte features

  • Most abundant and largest glial cell; star-shaped ("astro").
  • Can divide → astrogliosis (reactive proliferation).
  • Protoplasmic (gray matter) have specialized endfeet; fibrous in white matter.
  • Processes contact neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, axons, vessels and capillaries.
  • One astrocyte can contact ~10⁵ synapses.
  • Astrocytes produce glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), an intermediate filament: the marker that identifies them.

The four big jobs: Grow, Guide, Guard, Govern

JobWhat it means
Guide (development)Radial glia form scaffolding that migrating neurons climb along.
Grow (growth factors)Secrete growth factors that regulate neuron morphology, proliferation, differentiation and survival.
Guard (physical barrier)
  • Blood–brain barrier with CNS endothelial cells → keeps out proteins and charged molecules.
  • Glia limiting membrane: astrocyte endfeet + pial cells against the pia mater.
  • Scar formation after injury (walls off damaged tissue, especially if the BBB is breached).
Govern (homeostasis)
  • Regulate extracellular ions (K⁺ buffering) needed for neuronal depolarization.
  • Control blood flow by signaling vessels when more O₂/glucose is needed (the basis of the fMRI signal).
  • Control water flow and metabolite removal.
  • Supply energy and substrates for neurotransmission.
  • Rapidly remove neurotransmitters from the cleft → recycle glutamate and GABA.

Astrocytes at the synapse

  • Processes sit beside the synapse and take up released glutamate and GABA.
  • Glutamate → glutamine (glutamine synthetase) → handed back to the neuron to remake transmitter.
  • Have NT receptors (respond with Ca²⁺ rises), but no action potentials.
CheckWhat two cell types together form the BBB, and what forms the glia limiting membrane?
BBB = CNS endothelial cells + astrocyte endfeet. Glia limiting membrane = astrocyte endfeet + pial cells at the brain surface.
Step 1 HY — astrocytes & the BBB
  • BBB = tight junctions of non-fenestrated endothelium + basement membrane + astrocyte foot processes.
  • Levodopa crosses (carrier), dopamine does not; carbidopa stays peripheral, which is the point of pairing them.
  • No BBB at circumventricular organs (area postrema → vomiting; OVLT → osmolarity; posterior pituitary, median eminence).
  • Tumor/infarct disrupts the BBB → vasogenic edema.
  • Hyperammonemia: astrocytes detoxify NH₃ into glutamine → swollen Alzheimer type II astrocytes in hepatic encephalopathy.
  • Injury → reactive gliosis: the CNS scars with astrocytes, not collagen.
Step 1 trap

Don't mix up the two astrocyte barriers: BBB (endfeet + endothelium, around vessels) vs glia limiting membrane (endfeet + pia, at the brain surface). And GFAP marks astrocytes; myelin basic protein marks CNS myelin; neurofilament marks neurons.

Memory tool

Grow, Guide, Guard, Govern: growth factors, migration scaffolding, barriers/scar, homeostasis. The star (astro) is the stage manager: sets the stage (barrier), feeds the actors (energy) and sweeps up after each scene (glutamate/GABA, K⁺).

Buzzwords
"GFAP-positive" → astrocyte origin "endfeet" → protoplasmic astrocytes / BBB "radial glia" → neuronal migration scaffold "astrogliosis / glial scar" → reactive astrocytes "tripartite synapse" → astrocyte at the cleft
Q4. What are oligodendrocytes, and what is myelin made of?
Interfascicular oligodendrocytePerineuronal oligodendrocyte
WhereWhite matterGray matter
Myelinates?Yes: the main job of oligodendrocytesNo: surrounds neuronal cell bodies
Proposed roleMyelin → fast conductionSupport neurons via secreted soluble molecules (growth factors?)
  • Found in both gray and white matter; can divide.
  • One oligodendrocyte myelinates several axons (one internode on each; up to dozens) ↔ a PNS Schwann cell myelinates one internode of one axon.
  • Myelin = oligodendrocyte plasma membrane wrapped repeatedly and tightly around an axon segment (a myelin segment).
    • Protein + phospholipid with a high lipid:protein ratio (~80% lipid : 20% protein).
    • Contains proteins unique to CNS myelin, e.g., myelin basic protein (MBP), used as an identifier.
    • Insulates the axon → high resistance → impulses travel quickly.
CheckA section of gray matter shows oligodendrocytes clustered around neuron cell bodies. Are they making myelin?
No. Those are perineuronal oligodendrocytes: they hug cell bodies and are thought to support neurons with secreted molecules. Myelin comes from interfascicular (white-matter) oligodendrocytes.
CheckWhich protein is unique to CNS myelin and later becomes an autoimmune target?
Myelin basic protein (MBP).
Step 1 HY — oligodendrocyte vs Schwann cell
  • Oligodendrocyte: CNS, neuroectoderm, many axons, "fried egg" look on H&E; destroyed in MS, PML, leukodystrophies.
  • Schwann cell: PNS, neural crest, one axon segment, S-100+; target in Guillain–Barré; tumor = schwannoma.
  • Schwann cells also promote axon regeneration; oligodendrocyte myelin debris inhibits it.
Step 1 trap

"Demyelinating disease + CNS" = oligodendrocyte (MS, PML). "Demyelinating disease + ascending weakness, areflexia" = Schwann cell (Guillain–Barré). Same process, different glia, different nervous system.

Q5. How does myelin make conduction fast? (internodes, nodes, saltatory conduction)
  • The sheath is not continuous: each myelinated stretch = an internode; the bare gap between = a node of Ranvier.
  • Internode: insulated, high resistance, few channels → current flows down the axon instead of leaking out.
  • Node: lower resistance, packed with voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → the action potential is regenerated here (K⁺ channels cluster nearby).
  • The AP "leaps" node → node = saltatory conduction (Latin saltus, to leap).
  • Payoff: faster and cheaper. Without myelin, channels (and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase work) would be needed along the whole axon.
CheckAn axon of the same diameter loses its myelin. Predict conduction velocity and energy use.
Velocity falls (current leaks out of a low-resistance membrane; no saltatory jumps, sometimes complete conduction block) and energy cost rises (channels and pumps needed along the whole axon).
Step 1 HY

Myelin ↑ membrane resistance and ↓ capacitance → ↑ length constant → faster conduction. Demyelination → slowed conduction / conduction block (e.g., MS: optic neuritis, internuclear ophthalmoplegia). Larger diameter also ↑ velocity.

Memory tool

Saltatory = salta (Spanish/Latin "jump"). Picture a frog hopping from lily pad (node) to lily pad, skipping the insulated water (internode).

Q6. What are microglia and how do they switch on?

Microglia features

  • Smallest glia; in both gray and white matter; can divide.
  • The resident immune cells of the brain (macrophage-like).
  • Actively migrate to monitor the CNS; survey for damage/infection and phagocytose debris.
  • Hallmark: rapid activation in response to pathology.
  • Surface receptors bind viral, bacterial and other pathogen molecules → activation.
  • Development + plasticity: clear cells dying by normal programmed cell death and prune synapses throughout life.

CheckWhich glial cell removes excess synapses during development?
Microglia, by synaptic pruning (phagocytosing spines/synapses). They also clear developmentally dying neurons.
Step 1 HY — origin

Microglia are the odd ones out: mesoderm-derived (yolk-sac macrophage lineage), whereas astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and ependymal cells come from neuroectoderm, and Schwann/satellite cells from neural crest.

Memory tool

M1 = 1st responder: attacks (toxic). M2 = 2nd: cleans up and repairs (protective). Ramified = "branches out to watch"; amoeboid = "rolls up its sleeves to eat".

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A researcher knocks out the glutamate transporters on astrocytes in mice. What happens to synaptic glutamate, and why might neurons die?
Glutamate lingers in the cleft → excitotoxicity. Astrocytes normally clear glutamate rapidly (and recycle it as glutamine); without uptake, NMDA/AMPA receptors are over-stimulated → Ca²⁺ overload → neuron death.
A2A drug is a large, highly charged molecule. Will it reach the brain parenchyma from the bloodstream? Which structure stops it?
No. The BBB (endothelial tight junctions supported by astrocyte endfeet) restricts proteins and charged molecules; small lipid-soluble drugs cross, charged ones do not.
A3A single oligodendrocyte dies. Why can this affect more axons than the death of a single Schwann cell?
One oligodendrocyte myelinates internodes on several axons; a Schwann cell supplies one internode of one axon. So each oligodendrocyte loss demyelinates multiple axons at once.
A4After a contusion, brain tissue around the lesion becomes densely positive for GFAP over the next weeks. Interpret.
Reactive astrogliosis forming a glial scar. Astrocytes proliferate and wall off the injury (especially where the BBB is damaged); GFAP marks them.

LO 2Glia in neurologic diseaseTumors, demyelination and neuroinflammation

By the end: you can link each disease to the glial cell at fault: astrocytes → gliomas/GBM; oligodendrocytes → MS, PML and other myelin injury; microglia → HIV, neurodegeneration, secondary stroke injury and meningitis.
Q1. Which glial cells form tumors, and what is glioblastoma?
  • Gliomas can arise from almost any glial cell (they can divide → can divide badly).
  • Astrocytomas are the most common glial tumor.
    • Graded I–IV by degree of differentiation (better differentiated = lower grade).
  • Glioblastoma (multiforme), GBM = malignant grade IV astrocytoma; poor survival.

Glioblastoma astrocyte

Adult, cerebral hemispheres; can cross the corpus callosum ("butterfly glioma"); ring enhancement; pseudopalisading necrosis + vascular proliferation; GFAP+. Median survival ~1 yr.

Pilocytic astrocytoma astrocyte

Child, cerebellum; cystic with mural nodule; Rosenthal fibers, eosinophilic granular bodies; GFAP+; benign (grade I).

Oligodendroglioma oligodendrocyte

Adult, frontal lobe, seizures, often calcified; "fried egg" cells, "chicken-wire" capillaries; 1p/19q co-deletion.

Ependymoma ependymal

Child, 4th ventricle → hydrocephalus; perivascular pseudorosettes.

Schwannoma Schwann

CN VIII at cerebellopontine angle → hearing loss, tinnitus; S-100+; bilateral = NF2.

CheckWhat is the most common glial tumor overall, and what is its most malignant form?
Astrocytoma; the most malignant is GBM (grade IV astrocytoma).
Step 1 HY — glial tumors
  • GFAP+ tumor = astrocytic (GBM, pilocytic astrocytoma).
  • GBM: pseudopalisading necrosis, butterfly glioma, ring-enhancing; Rx resection + radiation + temozolomide (+ bevacizumab).
  • Current classification: "glioblastoma" is reserved for IDH-wildtype grade 4 adult tumors; boards still say GBM.
  • Adults: mostly supratentorial (GBM, meningioma, metastases). Children: mostly infratentorial (pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma, ependymoma).
Step 1 trap

A ring-enhancing lesion is not automatically GBM: in an AIDS patient think toxoplasmosis or primary CNS lymphoma; with fever think abscess; multiple at gray–white junction think metastases. Also: Rosenthal fibers = pilocytic (benign), pseudopalisading = GBM (malignant).

Step 1 HY — demyelination

PML = Polyoma Melts Lipid (myelin): JC virus eats the white-matter wiring in the immunosuppressed. MS = Myelin Spots hugging the ventricles.

Step 1 HY — HIV in the brain

HIV enters the CNS in infected monocytes ("Trojan horse") and infects microglia/macrophages (CD4 + CCR5); infected microglia fuse into multinucleated giant cells with microglial nodules. Step 1 trap

In HIV-associated dementia the cell infected is the microglia, not the neuron. In PML the cell infected is the oligodendrocyte (JC virus). Both happen in AIDS, so read the stem for "multinucleated giant cells" (HIV) vs "demyelinating white-matter lesions" (PML).

Q4. Board extension: in what order do neurons and glia respond after an ischemic injury?

This ties the whole cast together: neurons die first (highest metabolic demand), then neutrophils, microglia/macrophages clean up, and astrocytes scar.

Step 1 HY

Most hypoxia-sensitive neurons: hippocampal CA1 pyramidal cells, cerebellar Purkinje cells, neocortex layers 3, 5, 6 (laminar necrosis). Brain infarcts undergo liquefactive necrosis.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A 58-year-old with headaches and new seizures has a mass crossing the corpus callosum. Biopsy shows necrosis surrounded by lined-up tumor cells. Cell of origin and stain?
Astrocyte; GFAP+ glioblastoma. Butterfly pattern + pseudopalisading necrosis = GBM, a grade IV astrocytoma.
A2A patient with MS has been on natalizumab for 3 years and develops progressive hemiparesis and cognitive decline with new non-enhancing white-matter lesions. What happened?
PML from JC virus reactivation, destroying oligodendrocytes. Natalizumab blocks immune-cell entry into the CNS, which lowers MS relapses but removes immune surveillance of JC virus.
A3Perfusion is restored 3 h after a stroke, but the infarct keeps enlarging over the next days. Which cells and molecules contribute?
Activated (M1-like) microglia releasing TNF-α and IL-1β → secondary neuron death.
A4In pneumococcal meningitis, why does inflammation itself make things worse?
Microglial TNF-α and IL-1β open the BBB → more leukocytes flood in, amplifying inflammation and injury (the rationale for adjunctive dexamethasone).

LO 3How a neuron is builtCytoskeleton, dendrites, soma, axon hillock, axon, terminal and synapse

By the end: you can trace a signal through every part of a neuron, say what each part is specialized for, classify neurons four ways, and predict how a neuron reacts when its axon is cut.
Q1. What makes neurons special compared with glia?
  • Main job: receive, process and send information.
  • Structurally specialized for long-distance electrical signaling and communication via synapses.
  • Scale: ~100 billion neurons, ~100 trillion synapses.
  • Never work alone: organized into neural circuits that together produce behavior (movement, learning, memory). "Brain cells fire in patterns."
  • Do not divide once mature and differentiated.
  • Highly metabolically active: need lots of O₂, ATP and glucose.
CheckWhy are neurons the first cells to die in global hypoxia?
They have the highest metabolic demand (O₂, glucose, ATP for pumps and synaptic activity) and little reserve, and they cannot divide to replace losses.
Step 1 HY

Neurons are permanent (post-mitotic) cells, like cardiac myocytes; lost neurons are replaced by gliosis. Irreversible ischemic injury → "red neurons" at 12–24 h (shrunken, eosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknotic nucleus, Nissl lost).

Step 1 HY — cytoskeleton

  • Intermediate filament stains: neurofilament (neurons), GFAP (glia/astrocytes), vimentin (mesenchyme), desmin (muscle), cytokeratin (epithelium).

Q5. What do dendrites and dendritic spines do?
  • Extensions of the soma that are not myelinated; usually extensively branched ("tree-like").
    • Primary dendrites branch off the soma; secondary dendrites branch off those.
  • Receive signals via postsynaptic receptors for neurotransmitters; information travels distal → proximal and converges on the soma.
  • Dendritic spines = small protrusions.
    • Spine heads are the major site of postsynaptic ionotropic glutamate (excitatory) signaling.
    • Mature spines are mushroom-shaped (flat head, short shaft) → more surface for receptors.
    • Spine number and shape change with activity (plasticity).
  • Clinical: autism is associated with more thin, immature spines → neurocognitive changes.
CheckWhat is the mature spine shape, and what neurotransmitter dominates at spine heads?
Mushroom (flat head, short shaft); glutamate via ionotropic receptors (AMPA/NMDA).
Step 1 HY

Fragile X syndrome (FMR1 CGG repeat) also shows excess long, thin, immature spines, the classic board example of a "dendritic spine disorder"; it is the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability and a known cause of autism.

Memory tool

Spine → glutamate → excitation. A grown-up spine "mushrooms": big flat head to hold lots of receptors.

Q6. Why does the neuronal cell body look the way it does?

The soma is the support and metabolic center. Neurons make huge amounts of protein for a huge cell, so the soma looks like a factory:

CheckA Nissl stain shows the soma and proximal dendrites but not the axon. Why?
Nissl dyes stain rough ER/ribosomes, which are present in the soma and dendrites but absent from the axon hillock and axon.
Step 1 HY — Nissl & axon injury
  • Nissl substance = RER; in soma + dendrites, not in axon or axon hillock.
  • Axon cut → chromatolysis in the soma (peaks ~1–2 wk): soma swells, Nissl disperses, nucleus moves to the periphery = ↑ protein synthesis for repair.
  • Distal axon → Wallerian degeneration; proximal stump retracts and sprouts.
Step 1 trap

Chromatolysis is a reaction to axonal injury (reversible, repair mode), not cell death. Loss of Nissl with a shrunken, eosinophilic soma and pyknotic nucleus = red neuron (irreversible ischemia). Swollen vs shrunken is the tell.

Q7. What does the axon do, from hillock to terminal?
  • Arises from the soma at the axon hillock; ends at the axon terminal (presynaptic terminal).
    • Most neurons have a single axon.
    • The terminal is the site of small-molecule NT synthesis (e.g., ACh, glutamate, GABA, monoamines); neuropeptides are made in the soma and shipped down.
  • Transmits information via action potentials (rapid change in charge across the axonal membrane) to other neurons or effector cells (e.g., muscle).
  • Axoplasm: dense bundles of microtubules and neurofilaments (structure + transport of metabolites and organelles), smooth ER (lipid synthesis), mitochondria. No RER.
  • Myelin: CNS axons by oligodendrocytes, PNS axons by Schwann cells; others unmyelinated. Myelin ↑ conduction (internodes, nodes rich in ion channels, saltatory conduction).
CheckWhere is the action potential initiated, and why there?
At the axon hillock / initial segment: it has the highest density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, so it has the lowest threshold.
Step 1 trap

"Where are neurotransmitters made?" Small molecules → in the terminal (enzymes delivered anterogradely). Neuropeptides → in the soma (need RER/Golgi), then shipped. Don't answer "soma" for acetylcholine.

Q8. What is a chemical synapse made of, and where can synapses form?
  • Synapses let neurons work in circuits, transferring information neuron → neuron.
  • Three parts: presynaptic element (axon terminal with vesicles, mitochondria) → synaptic cleft → postsynaptic element (neurotransmitter receptors).
  • Postsynaptic contacts can be on dendrites (axodendritic), the cell body (axosomatic), along an axon or at axon terminals (axoaxonic). Many synapse types exist.
  • Add the astrocyte process and you have the tripartite synapse (LO 1).

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A drug selectively blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels at the axon initial segment. What happens to the neuron's output?
Fewer or no action potentials are initiated, even with normal dendritic input, because the hillock/initial segment is the trigger zone.
A2A neuropathology study of a child with autism examines cortical dendrites. What spine change do you expect?
More thin, immature spines (fewer mature mushroom spines) → impaired connections and neurocognitive changes.
A3Ten days after a sciatic nerve laceration, the motor neuron cell bodies in the anterior horn look swollen with peripheral nuclei and pale cytoplasm. Interpret.
Chromatolysis: the soma disperses its Nissl and ramps up protein synthesis to regrow the axon. A reparative, not dying, response.
A4Which organelle would you find in a neuron's soma but not in its axon, and what stain shows it?
Rough ER (Nissl substance); shown by a Nissl stain (e.g., cresyl violet).

LO 4Axonal transportKinesin out, dynein back, and the pathogens that hitch a ride

By the end: you can explain why neurons need transport, name the motor and cargo for each direction, and predict which infections and toxins use it.
Q1. Why do neurons need a dedicated transport system?
  • Proteins (enzymes, receptors, neuropeptides) and organelles are made in the soma, yet the terminal may be up to a meter away.
  • The terminal and dendrites also need to send signals and worn-out material back.
  • So transport is critical for neuron function, is bidirectional and needs specialized motor proteins walking on microtubules.

Retrograde, dynein. Neurotrophins travel from the terminal back to the soma.
Memory tool

Kinesin Kicks cargo out to the terminal; Dynein Drags it back Down to the soma. Also: "retrograde returns".

Step 1 HY — speeds & defects
  • Fast transport (~200–400 mm/day) carries membrane-bound vesicles/organelles; slow transport (~1–8 mm/day) carries cytoskeletal proteins and soluble enzymes (sets the ~1 mm/day pace of nerve regrowth).
  • Dynein also powers cilia/flagella: dynein arm defect = primary ciliary dyskinesia (Kartagener).
  • Vincristine (blocks polymerization) and paclitaxel (blocks depolymerization) both cause peripheral neuropathy, since long axons depend most on transport.
Q3. How do pathogens use retrograde transport?

Rabies virus

Animal bite → replicates in muscle → binds nicotinic ACh receptor, enters nerve terminals → retrograde to spinal cord → brain → fatal encephalitis. Negri bodies (Purkinje, hippocampus). Long incubation (weeks–months); shorter for bites near the head.

Herpes viruses (HSV, VZV)

Skin/mucosa → sensory terminals → retrograde to ganglion (trigeminal, dorsal root) → latency. Reactivation travels anterograde back → dermatomal lesions (cold sores, shingles).

Tetanus toxin

Wound → toxin enters motor terminals → retrograde to spinal cord → taken up by inhibitory Renshaw interneurons → cleaves synaptobrevin → blocks GABA/glycine release → spastic paralysis, trismus, risus sardonicus.

Retrograde axonal transport (dynein) from sensory terminals in the lip to the neuron's cell body in the ganglion.
Step 1 trap

Botulinum toxin acts at the peripheral presynaptic terminal (blocks ACh release at the NMJ → flaccid paralysis); tetanus toxin travels retrograde into the CNS to block inhibitory release (→ spastic paralysis). Both cleave SNARE proteins; the location differs.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1A mutation impairs kinesin. What fails to reach the axon terminal?
Anterograde cargo: biosynthetic enzymes (including those that make small-molecule NTs), organelles like mitochondria, receptors and neuropeptides → the terminal starves.
A2A hiker bitten on the ankle by a bat has symptoms months later; another bitten on the face has symptoms in weeks. Explain.
Distance of retrograde transport. Rabies virus must travel by dynein up the axon to the CNS; a shorter path (face) means a shorter incubation.
A3A patient with lymphoma receiving vincristine develops numbness in the feet first. Why the longest nerves first?
Vincristine disrupts microtubules → impaired axonal transport; the longest axons (to the feet) depend most on transport, so they fail first (length-dependent neuropathy).
A4A farmer with a puncture wound develops jaw stiffness and painful spasms. How did the toxin get to its target?
Retrograde axonal transport of tetanus toxin from motor terminals to the spinal cord, where it blocks GABA/glycine release from inhibitory interneurons.

2A 34-year-old woman with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis has been treated with natalizumab for 3 years. She develops progressive right arm weakness, aphasia and confusion over 2 months. MRI shows multiple non-enhancing lesions in subcortical white matter without mass effect. Which cell is the primary target of the responsible agent?

B. Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy: JC virus (human polyomavirus 2) destroys oligodendrocytes → multifocal demyelination; natalizumab (and AIDS) are classic setups. A/C: HIV targets microglia, not JC virus or astrocytes. D: HSV-1 causes temporal lobe hemorrhagic encephalitis. E: Schwann cells are PNS; CMV causes polyradiculitis/retinitis in AIDS.

3A 9-year-old boy is bitten on the calf by a raccoon. Seven weeks later he develops fever, agitation, hydrophobia and pharyngeal spasms. The agent traveled from the bite site to the central nervous system mainly by which mechanism?

E. Rabies virus replicates in muscle, binds the nicotinic ACh receptor, enters nerve terminals and rides dynein retrogradely to the spinal cord and brain. B is the wrong direction (kinesin carries cargo away from the soma). A, C, D are not the classic route; the long incubation reflects slow travel up the axon.

4A mouse line carries a loss-of-function mutation in a microtubule plus-end-directed motor protein expressed in motor neurons. Which finding is most expected in these neurons?

A. Plus-end motor = kinesin = anterograde: delivers organelles (mitochondria), enzymes, receptors and vesicle proteins to the terminal. B and C describe retrograde (dynein) failure. D: axons never contain Nissl (RER). E: neuropeptides are still synthesized in the soma; they just cannot be shipped.

5A 27-year-old woman has painful monocular vision loss that resolves, then months later develops numbness of the legs. MRI shows ovoid periventricular white-matter lesions perpendicular to the ventricles. CSF shows oligoclonal IgG bands. Which process best explains her lesions?

C. MS: idiopathic inflammatory demyelination; with a compromised BBB, infiltrating peripheral immune cells react to CNS-unique myelin proteins (MBP) → oligodendrocyte and myelin loss. A = Guillain–Barré (PNS). B = PML (immunosuppressed, progressive). D = HIV encephalitis. E = metachromatic leukodystrophy (children, progressive, not relapsing).

6A patient with Parkinson disease is given levodopa with carbidopa. Dopamine itself is ineffective when given intravenously because it cannot enter the brain. Which structure is chiefly responsible for keeping dopamine out?

A. The BBB is built by CNS endothelium (tight junctions, no fenestrae) with basement membrane and astrocyte endfeet; it blocks charged/polar molecules like dopamine. B lines the brain surface beneath the pia, not capillaries. C insulates axons. D lines ventricles (CSF–brain interface is leaky). E: microglia are immune cells, not barrier cells.

7A 70-year-old man has a left MCA ischemic stroke and receives thrombolysis with good reperfusion. Over the next 3 days, follow-up imaging shows enlargement of the infarct. Experimental blockade of TNF-α and IL-1β limits this expansion. Which cells are the main source of these cytokines in the brain parenchyma?

D. Activated (M1-like, amoeboid) microglia release pro-inflammatory, neurotoxic TNF-α and IL-1β → secondary neuron death after stroke and trauma. A: perineuronal oligodendrocytes support neurons. B: astrocytes form the later scar. C/E line ventricles and make CSF.

8A 66-year-old woman is resuscitated after a 12-minute cardiac arrest and dies 20 hours later. Microscopy of the hippocampal CA1 region would most likely show which change in pyramidal neurons?

B. Red neurons appear 12–24 h after irreversible hypoxic injury (Nissl lost, eosinophilic, pyknotic). A = chromatolysis after axonal injury (reparative). C = macrophages at 3–5 days. D = scar after 2+ weeks. E = Alzheimer disease, not acute ischemia.

9A 25-year-old man has a complete laceration of the ulnar nerve at the elbow that is surgically repaired. Two weeks later, which change is most likely in the cell bodies of the injured motor neurons in the spinal cord?

C. Chromatolysis: the soma gears up protein synthesis (RER redistributes, nucleus moves aside) to regenerate the axon. A describes red/dying neurons. B is rabies. D: myelin debris is cleared distally (Wallerian degeneration) by Schwann cells/macrophages. E: mature neurons do not divide.

10In a myelinated CNS axon, action potentials travel quickly because the impulse is regenerated only at discrete points. At which location is the density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels along the myelinated portion of the axon greatest?

E. Nodes are unmyelinated, low-resistance gaps packed with Na⁺ channels; the AP "leaps" node to node (saltatory conduction). A: internodes are insulated with few channels. B: glia do not fire APs. C: dendrites rely mostly on ligand-gated receptors. D: the active zone is rich in Ca²⁺ channels for release, not AP propagation.

11Golgi-stained cortical neurons from a patient with a neurodevelopmental disorder show increased numbers of long, thin dendritic protrusions and few spines with large flat heads on short necks. Which statement about the missing spine type is correct?

C. Mature spines are mushroom-shaped (flat head, short shaft); spine heads host postsynaptic ionotropic glutamate receptors. Excess thin immature spines are seen in autism (and fragile X). A: filopodia are immature; spines are mainly excitatory. B: spines are on dendrites; axons lack Nissl. D: dendrites are unmyelinated. E describes the abundant thin spines, not the missing ones.

12A 42-year-old man with untreated HIV (CD4 count 60/µL) has slowly progressive memory loss, apathy and psychomotor slowing. Brain biopsy shows microglial nodules and multinucleated giant cells. Which statement best describes the cellular basis of his disease?

B. HIV targets microglia (and macrophages); infected cells fuse into multinucleated giant cells and drive inflammation and neuron death (HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder). A: neurons are not productively infected. C = PML (focal deficits, white-matter lesions). D = toxoplasmosis (focal ring-enhancing lesions). E = astrocytoma.

13An unvaccinated 50-year-old man steps on a rusty nail. Ten days later he has jaw stiffness, a fixed grimace and generalized muscle spasms triggered by noise. The responsible toxin reaches its target cells by which route, and what does it block there?

D. Tetanus toxin enters motor terminals and travels retrogradely (dynein) to the spinal cord, where it cleaves synaptobrevin in Renshaw/inhibitory interneurons → loss of GABA/glycine inhibition → spastic paralysis. A and E describe botulinum toxin (flaccid paralysis at the NMJ; not CNS transport). B and C are not mechanisms of tetanus.
