Cerebral Edema, Herniation & CNS Trauma

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Explain how the three kinds of brain edema form, and how swelling plus rising intracranial pressure starve the brain of blood flow and disturb its function.
  2. LO 2Recognize the bedside signs of raised intracranial pressure and predict what each herniation pattern compresses and which deficit results.
  3. LO 3Sort traumatic brain injuries (concussion, contusion, diffuse axonal injury, hemorrhage) into a working classification and match each to how the patient looks.
  4. LO 4Tell epidural, subdural, subarachnoid and intraparenchymal bleeds apart by the vessel torn, the space filled, the CT shape and the time course.
  5. LO 5Predict the injury pattern from the way the head was hit (impact vs rotation, fall vs blow, penetrating vs blast) using the underlying anatomy.
  6. LO 6Describe how trauma damages arteries and veins of the CNS: tears, dissections, traumatic aneurysms and the bleeds they produce.

LO 1Cerebral edema & intracranial pressureWhy a swollen brain in a rigid skull loses its blood supply

By the end: you can name the edema type from a one-line vignette, recite the ICP cut-offs (10, 20, 40, 60 mmHg), use CPP = MAP − ICP to predict ischemia, and explain the Monro-Kellie "closed box".
Q1. What is the difference between "edema" and "brain swelling"?
  • Edema = an increase in brain water content.
  • Brain swelling = edema + vascular congestion (more blood volume in the brain). In trauma it is mainly edema.
  • Swelling is common in TBI and is the main driver of raised ICP → the most common cause of death in severe TBI.
  • Swelling can be:
    • Focal → around a contusion or intracerebral hemorrhage; usually vasogenic (barrier breakdown).
    • Diffuse, one hemisphere → typically with an acute subdural hematoma; it persists even after the clot is removed.
    • Diffuse, both hemispheres → now less frequent because modern care aggressively prevents hypoxia and ischemia.
CheckA surgeon evacuates an acute subdural hematoma, yet the hemisphere underneath keeps bulging. Is that expected?
Yes. Unilateral diffuse swelling with acute SDH characteristically persists after evacuation; the hematoma is not the only source of mass effect.

Step 1 HY — edema on imaging & treatment
  • Cytotoxic = restricted diffusion (bright on DWI) within minutes of a stroke.
  • Vasogenic = white-matter, finger-like T2/FLAIR signal around a ring-enhancing mass; responds to dexamethasone.
  • Steroids are not used for traumatic brain swelling (no benefit, possibly harmful).
  • Rapid correction of hyponatremia → osmotic demyelination; untreated acute hyponatremia → cytotoxic swelling.
Memory tool — "In, Out, Around"

Cytotoxic = water in the Cell. Vasogenic = water out of the Vessel. Interstitial = water around the ventricles (from the Inside of the ventricle).

Q3. Why does a new mass raise ICP, and why does pressure suddenly skyrocket?
  • The skull is a solid box. Its contents (brain + blood + CSF) have a fixed total volume = Monro-Kellie doctrine.
  • A growing mass (hematoma, tumor, abscess, diffuse swelling) must displace something:
    • First CSF (pushed into the spinal sac) and venous blood (squeezed out) → ICP barely changes = compensation.
    • Once these buffers are used up, each extra mL → a steep rise in ICP = decompensation.
  • Pressure is then unequal between compartments → a pressure differential → tissue herniates into the neighboring compartment (LO 2).
  • ICP thresholds (the dashed lines on the curve): normal 0–10 mmHg → prolonged >20 = abnormal → >40 = neurologic dysfunction + compromised cerebral circulation → >60 = fatal.
  • Raised ICP from swelling = the most common cause of death in severe TBI.
CheckWhy can a patient look fine for hours with a slowly enlarging clot and then crash within minutes?
On the flat part of the curve, displacement of CSF and venous blood hides the clot. Once at the knee, a few more mL send ICP up steeply → herniation.
Q4. How does high ICP actually injure the brain? (perfusion)
  • Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) = MAP − ICP. As ICP rises, CPP falls.
  • Autoregulation normally keeps flow constant over a wide pressure range, but injured brain loses it.
  • Low CPP → global ischemia → cytotoxic edema → even higher ICP → the vicious cycle.
  • If ICP approaches MAP, CPP → 0 → no cerebral perfusion = brain death.

Two routes to diffuse ischemic injury after TBI

1 · Reduced perfusion

  • Brain swelling (↑ICP)
  • Cardiorespiratory arrest
  • Profound hypotension from other injuries (long-bone fractures, solid-organ bleeding)

2 · Metabolic mismatch

  • Demand rises sharply while O₂ delivery is limited
  • Seizures, excitotoxic stress
CheckMAP 80 mmHg, ICP 45 mmHg. What is the CPP, and why worry?
CPP = 35 mmHg (normal ≈ 60–80). Well below the autoregulatory range → ischemia, and ICP is already in the "neurologic dysfunction" zone (>40).
Step 1 HY — lowering ICP
  • Head of bed ~30° → better venous drainage.
  • Hyperventilation → ↓PaCO₂ → cerebral vasoconstriction → ↓ blood volume → ↓ICP within minutes. Short-term only (prolonged → ischemia).
  • Mannitol or hypertonic saline → osmotic pull of water out of the brain.
  • Treat the cause: evacuate the hematoma, drain CSF (ventriculostomy), decompressive craniectomy.
  • Hypercapnia, hypoxia → cerebral vasodilation → ↑ICP. Boards often call normal adult ICP <15 mmHg; treat when sustained >20.
Step 1 trap

CPP depends on both numbers. A hypotensive trauma patient (low MAP) can become ischemic with only a modestly raised ICP. "Fix the blood pressure" is part of treating a head injury.

Memory tool

ICP ladder 10 · 20 · 40 · 60 = "fine · flag · failing · fatal".

Buzzwords
"skull is a closed box" → Monro-Kellie "restricted diffusion, intact BBB" → cytotoxic "finger-like white-matter edema, better on steroids" → vasogenic "periventricular lucency in hydrocephalus" → interstitial "MAP − ICP" → cerebral perfusion pressure

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1Thirty minutes after cardiac arrest with successful resuscitation, MRI shows diffuse bright DWI in cortex and deep grey matter. Edema type and mechanism?
Cytotoxic. Global ischemia → ATP loss → Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase failure → water moves into neurons and glia; the barrier is still intact.
A2A child with aqueductal stenosis has huge lateral ventricles with a halo of low density around them on CT. What is the halo?
Interstitial (hydrocephalic) edema: raised intraventricular pressure forces CSF across the ependyma into periventricular white matter.
A3Predict: a TBI patient is intubated and accidentally hypoventilated (PaCO₂ rises from 35 to 55). What happens to ICP and why?
ICP rises. CO₂ dilates cerebral arterioles → ↑ cerebral blood volume → in a closed skull already near the "knee", pressure jumps and CPP falls.
A4A trauma patient with a femur fracture and splenic laceration has BP 70/40 and a small contusion. Why might the brain show widespread ischemic damage at autopsy?
Reduced perfusion from profound hypotension due to other body injuries: low MAP → low CPP → diffuse ischemic injury, independent of the contusion size.

LO 2Raised ICP & herniation syndromesWhat moves where, and what it crushes on the way

By the end: you can list the signs of raised ICP, draw the herniation sites, and turn "blown pupil + hemiparesis on the same side" into a diagnosis with a mechanism.
Q1. How does a patient with raised ICP look before anything herniates?
  • Headache (worse lying down / on waking), vomiting (often without nausea), papilledema.
  • ↓ Level of consciousness → confusion, drowsiness, then coma.
  • CN VI (abducens) palsy → lateral gaze palsy, a false-localizing sign (the nerve is stretched against the petrous ligament).
  • Late: Cushing triad = hypertension (widened pulse pressure) + bradycardia + irregular respirations.

CheckWhy does the heart rate fall while the blood pressure soars?
The ischemic brainstem fires a sympathetic surge (↑BP to restore CPP); carotid/aortic baroreceptors sense the hypertension and trigger a vagal reflex bradycardia.
Step 1 HY — Cushing triad

Hypertension + bradycardia + irregular (Cheyne-Stokes / ataxic) respirations = impending herniation. Do not lumbar puncture a patient with papilledema or a mass: removing CSF from below can pull the tonsils into the foramen magnum. Image first.

Step 1 trap — false-localizing signs

A CN VI palsy with raised ICP does not mean a lesion near the abducens nucleus, and Kernohan notch gives weakness on the wrong side. Both are pressure effects, not lesion sites.

Q2. Where can brain herniate, and what gets compressed at each site?

The falx cerebri (between hemispheres) and the tentorium cerebelli (roof over the cerebellum) split the cranium into compartments. A pressure differential pushes tissue under the falx, through the tentorial notch, or down the foramen magnum; vessels and nerves at these edges get notched, kinked or stretched → secondary infarcts, more edema, more swelling.

CheckRight temporal hematoma. Which pupil blows first, and which visual field is lost if the PCA is compressed?
Right pupil (right CN III). Right PCA → right occipital infarct → left homonymous hemianopia (often macular sparing).
Step 1 HY — the blown pupil

The parasympathetic pupillomotor fibers ride on the outside of CN III, so compression dilates the pupil first (fixed, unreactive), before ptosis and "down and out" gaze. Contrast: diabetic (ischemic) CN III palsy spares the pupil because the infarct hits the core. A blown pupil after head trauma = uncal herniation until proven otherwise; it points to the side of the mass.

Step 1 trap — which side is weak?

Usual uncal herniation squeezes the same-side peduncle → weakness on the opposite side. Kernohan squeezes the opposite peduncle → weakness on the same side as the blown pupil. If the vignette gives an ipsilateral blown pupil and ipsilateral hemiparesis, the answer is Kernohan notch, not a second lesion.

Memory tool — top to bottom

"Under, Over, Down, Out": under the falx (cingulate → ACA → leg), over the tentorium (uncus → III + PCA), down the middle (central → Duret), out the bottom (tonsils → medulla → death). Herniation follows the gaps in the dura.

Buzzwords
"fixed dilated pupil + coma after head injury" → uncal herniation "blown pupil and hemiparesis on the same side" → Kernohan notch "flame-shaped midline pontine hemorrhages" → Duret "contralateral leg weakness after frontal mass" → subfalcine, ACA "HTN, bradycardia, irregular breathing" → Cushing triad "grooved parahippocampal gyrus" → tentorial herniation
Q3. What do tonsillar herniation and Duret hemorrhages look like at autopsy?
  • Tonsillar: tonsils plug the foramen magnum → blocks CSF (↑ICP), tonsils undergo hemorrhagic necrosis, the medulla (respiratory + cardiovascular centers) becomes ischemic → pressure + ischemia → cardiopulmonary arrest.
  • Duret: brainstem is pushed down and elongated; the basilar artery is tethered to the circle of Willis, so its paramedian perforators are stretched and tear → midline hemorrhages in midbrain and pons. A secondary brainstem injury.
CheckA pontine hemorrhage in a patient who died of a huge hemispheric hematoma. Hypertensive bleed or Duret?
Duret: secondary to herniation from the supratentorial mass. A hypertensive pontine bleed is a primary event, without a supratentorial mass driving it.
Step 1 HY — tonsils outside trauma

Tonsillar descent is also chronic in Chiari I (tonsils >5 mm below the foramen magnum; headache, syringomyelia). Acute tonsillar herniation after an LP in a patient with a mass is the classic iatrogenic catastrophe.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A man with a growing right frontal tumor develops weakness of his left foot and leg, arm spared. Herniation and vessel?
Subfalcine (cingulate) herniation compressing the right ACA against the falx → medial frontal (leg) cortex infarct.
A2After a left temporal epidural hematoma, a patient has a fixed dilated left pupil and LEFT arm and leg weakness. Explain both.
Left uncal herniation → left CN III (blown pupil). The brainstem is shoved right, pressing the right peduncle against the tentorium (Kernohan notch) → left-sided weakness.
A3Predict: a patient with uncal herniation who survives develops a new visual problem. What and why?
Contralateral homonymous hemianopia: PCA kinked against the tentorial edge → occipital (calcarine) infarct.
A4Vital signs: BP 210/110, HR 44, irregular breathing in a patient with a large cerebellar hemorrhage. What is happening?
Cushing triad from raised posterior fossa pressure → impending tonsillar herniation with medullary compression. Emergency decompression is needed.

LO 3Classifying traumatic brain injuryConcussion · contusion · diffuse axonal injury · hemorrhage, and what each looks like at the bedside

By the end: you can sort any TBI as focal vs diffuse and primary vs secondary, score the three GCS domains, and distinguish concussion, contusion, DAI and CTE by mechanism, clinical course and pathology.
Q1. Why are there three different ways to classify TBI?

No single scheme captures everything, so three lenses are used together:

Clinical

Glasgow Coma Scale: the most widely used; weaker in mild injury and in children. Severe TBI adds metabolic profile, O₂ saturation, pH, glucose and CT/MRI.

Mechanistic

Impact, inertial loading, penetrating, blast (details in LO 5). Most real injuries mix contact + inertial forces.

Pathological

Anatomic: focal vs diffuse. Pathophysiologic: primary = immediate, direct consequence of the physical force; secondary = delayed and/or indirect. The split is partly artificial.

Focal injuryDiffuse injury
1 · Scalp bruising / lacerations1 · Ischemia (hypoperfusion, metabolic mismatch)
2 · Skull fractures (linear, basilar, depressed/comminuted, compound)2 · Diffuse traumatic axonal (and vascular) injury
3 · Contusions / lacerations of brain3 · Brain swelling
4 · Intracranial hemorrhage (EDH, SDH, SAH, ICH, IVH)
5 · Focal lesions from raised ICP (herniation, Duret, infarcts)
  • Know both lists: 5 focal (scalp, skull fracture, contusion/laceration, intracranial hemorrhage, raised-ICP lesions) and 3 diffuse (ischemia, axonal/vascular injury, swelling).
  • Epidemiology: TBI is the leading cause of death under 40 and accounts for 1–2% of all deaths; falls are the most common mechanism (~35%).
CheckClassify a Duret hemorrhage on both axes.
Focal (anatomically) and secondary (it results from herniation, not from the impact itself).

Q3. What exactly is a concussion?
  • An immediate, usually reversible episode of cerebral dysfunction after TBI, typically (not always) with brief loss of consciousness and memory loss. The mildest form of TBI.
  • Think TIA vs stroke: concussion is the transient, no-visible-lesion end; DAI is the devastating end of the same rotational spectrum.
  • Sport guidance has moved away from grading systems. In rare autopsies of people who died of other causes 2–99 days after a concussion, sparse β-APP-positive axonal injury was found → "reversible" may not mean "no structural damage".

Early (minutes–hours)

Headache, nausea/vomiting, dizziness, confusion, disorientation, poor attention, memory deficit, slurred speech, incoordination, emotional lability.

Delayed (days–weeks)

Persistent headache, blurred vision, tinnitus/hearing loss, light-headedness, poor concentration and memory, irritability, fatigue, light/noise intolerance, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbance.

  • Mental-status sequence after closed head injury: retrograde amnesia (events just before) → post-traumatic amnesia (coma/confusion afterward, cannot lay down new memories) → usually recovery of memory.
CheckA player cannot remember the play before the hit. Retrograde or anterograde?
Retrograde (before the injury). Inability to form new memories after the hit is anterograde / post-traumatic amnesia.
Q4. What is a contusion, where does it occur, and what does an old one look like?
  • Contusion = bruise of the brain surface: mechanical force damages small vessels → blood leaks into tissue. Worst at the crests of gyri (where the cortex hits bone).
  • Pia intact = contusion; if the pia is torn → laceration.
  • Caused by impact with acceleration/deceleration (e.g., a fall). Coup = under the impact; contrecoup = opposite side (LO 5).
  • Most common sites: tips of the temporal lobes (temporal poles) and inferior frontal lobes (orbital gyri): the brain rubs across the rough bony floor of the anterior and middle cranial fossae.
  • Contusions trigger focal vasogenic edema and can enlarge into intracerebral hematomas.
  • Old contusion = "plaques jaunes": depressed, yellow-brown (hemosiderin) gliotic plaques in the cortex, on gyral crests, with shrinking of the surrounding gyri → a common epileptic focus.
CheckWhy are contusions rare on the occipital lobes even after occipital impact?
The inner occipital skull and the tentorium are smooth; the frontal and temporal lobes sit on rough, ridged fossae, so they tear on deceleration. Occipital impact → frontal/temporal contrecoup.
Step 1 HY

Orbitofrontal contusions → personality change, disinhibition; temporal pole contusions → memory problems and post-traumatic epilepsy (plaques jaunes are the scar).

Q5. What is diffuse axonal injury (DAI), and how does it evolve under the microscope?
  • Cause: high-velocity rotational acceleration/deceleration of an unrestrained head (road traffic accidents, falls from a height). No skull impact needed.
  • Mechanism: shear, tensile and compressive strain deforms long white-matter tracts → immediate breakage of microtubules → axonal transport stops → transported material piles up → axon swells and later disconnects (secondary, delayed axotomy).
  • Sites: grey–white junction, corpus callosum (especially the splenium), white matter near the midline (internal capsule), dorsolateral rostral brainstem.
  • Clinical: comatose immediately, no lucid interval, remains unconscious, vegetative or severely disabled. CT may look nearly normal; MRI (DWI, susceptibility/T2*) shows punctate lesions.
  • Grades by coma length: mild = coma 6–24 h; moderate = >24 h without brainstem signs; severe = >24 h with brainstem signs (posturing).
  • Time course, in order: β-APP → varicosities (H&E, 12–24 h) → swellings/spheroids + microglial nodules (24 h–2 mo) → Wallerian degeneration, atrophy.
  • The β-APP window is quoted as within <2 hours with current techniques (older teaching: 4–5 hours). "Earliest finding" → β-APP either way.
  • The H&E image is white matter: oligodendrocytes present, neurons absent.
  • DAI is a severe injury; delayed swelling can push the brain into the foramen magnum → brainstem compression → respiratory failure.
CheckThe patient died 3 hours after the crash. Will H&E show axonal swellings? What test will?
Probably not on H&E (varicosities appear at 12–24 h, spheroids after ~24 h). β-APP immunostaining detects accumulation within <2 hours.
Step 1 trap

Comatose crash victim, nearly normal CT, no fracture, no hematoma → not "malingering" and not "concussion": it is DAI. Next best imaging = MRI. And DAI has no lucid interval (that's epidural).

Memory tool

Picture a spinning bowl of jelly with strings through it: rotation stretches the strings at the jelly–bowl interface (grey–white junction) and in the midline bridge (corpus callosum). β-APP = "Backed-up Axonal Post-office Parcels".

Q6. What is chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)?
  • First recognized in boxers as dementia pugilistica; now described in football, hockey, soccer and military personnel.
  • Cause not fully defined; probably repeated concussive or sub-concussive blows.
  • Clinical: mood disturbance, depression, impulsivity, memory loss → dementia, or parkinsonism / ALS-like disease; suicide risk.
  • Pathology: extensive hyperphosphorylated tau in neurons and glia (astrocytes), classically perivascular at the depths of sulci; sometimes TDP-43 inclusions (the protein is TDP-43).
    • Tau → also seen in Alzheimer disease.
    • TDP-43 → also seen in ALS and frontotemporal dementia.
CheckWhich two proteins accumulate in CTE, and which other diseases share them?
Tau (also Alzheimer disease, frontotemporal dementia, PSP) and TDP-43 (also ALS and frontotemporal lobar degeneration).
Buzzwords
"plaques jaunes" → old contusion "orbital gyri + temporal poles" → contusion sites "β-APP, axonal spheroids" → DAI "comatose from the start, normal CT" → DAI "retired boxer, dementia + parkinsonism" → CTE (dementia pugilistica) "perivascular tau at sulcal depths" → CTE

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A 27-year-old unrestrained passenger in a head-on crash is unresponsive at the scene with no scalp wound or skull fracture, reactive pupils and no withdrawal to pain. MRI shows a lesion in the splenium. Diagnosis and the autopsy finding if he dies weeks later?
Diffuse axonal injury. Autopsy: corpus callosum hemorrhage/discoloration, small deep hemorrhages, blurred grey–white junction; microscopy shows axonal swellings, microglial nodules, β-APP-positive axons.
A2An adult with post-traumatic seizures has yellow-brown, depressed scars on the crests of inferior frontal gyri at autopsy. What are they?
Plaques jaunes: old, healed contusions (hemosiderin + gliosis) that served as the seizure focus.
A3Predict: a football player is hit, briefly "sees stars", has a headache and nausea, CT is normal, and he recovers fully in a week. Category and most likely microscopic correlate if one exists?
Concussion (mild TBI). Imaging is normal; if anything, sparse β-APP axonal injury would be the hidden lesion.
A4Classify: (a) contusion, (b) brain swelling, (c) DAI, (d) subdural hematoma — focal or diffuse?
Focal: contusion, subdural hematoma. Diffuse: brain swelling, DAI.

LO 4Epidural vs subdural vs subarachnoid vs intraparenchymalVessel torn → space filled → CT shape → clinical clock

By the end: from a vignette you can name the bleed, the vessel, the CT shape and the time course, and explain why each one looks the way it does.
Q1. Which layer does each bleed sit in?

Work from the outside in: skull → (epidural space) → dura → (subdural space) → arachnoid → (subarachnoid space, CSF + surface arteries) → pia → brain. The epidural and subdural spaces are potential spaces: they exist only when blood forces them open.

CheckAt autopsy, blood on the brain surface cannot be wiped off with a sponge. Which space?
Subarachnoid: it lies under the arachnoid, in the sulci and cisterns. Subdural clot lifts off with the dura or wipes away.

Step 1 HY — the anatomy behind the vignette
  • Middle meningeal artery = branch of the maxillary artery, enters via the foramen spinosum, runs under the pterion (thinnest point where frontal, parietal, temporal and sphenoid meet).
  • Bridging veins run from the cortex to the superior sagittal sinus; brain atrophy (elderly, chronic alcohol use) stretches them → minor falls tear them. Infants: shaken baby.
  • Chronic SDH: weeks after trivial trauma, hypodense crescent, organizes with granulation tissue/neomembranes whose fragile vessels rebleed (mixed density); fluctuating confusion mimics dementia.
  • Spontaneous SAH = berry aneurysm (ACom most common; ADPKD, Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan, coarctation): "worst headache of my life", xanthochromic CSF, vasospasm days 3–10 (nimodipine). Trauma is still the most common cause of SAH overall.
  • Spontaneous ICH = hypertension (Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms of lenticulostriate arteries → putamen, thalamus, pons, cerebellum); lobar bleeds in the elderly → cerebral amyloid angiopathy.
Step 1 trap
  • "Lucid interval" = EDH, not SDH or DAI. But a lucid interval is not always present; absence does not exclude EDH.
  • Elderly + delayed confusion after a fall, no loss of consciousness → SDH, even if the fall seemed trivial or was not witnessed.
  • "Thunderclap headache" without trauma → aneurysmal SAH, not traumatic SAH.
  • Basal ganglia hemorrhage in a hypertensive → spontaneous ICH; a pontine bleed with a supratentorial mass → Duret.
Memory tool

EDH = "Lens" → "Lens, Lucid, Lateral (temporal), Lethal fast". SDH = "Crescent moon" → "Slow, Senior, Shaken baby, Spans sutures". Artery is above the dura (epidural); veins are venturing across the subdural space.

A1A 19-year-old snowboarder in a baseball cap hits a tree, is briefly unconscious, then wants another run; en route to the ED he becomes comatose. X-ray: linear left temporoparietal fracture. Lesion?
Laceration of the middle meningeal artery → epidural hematoma. Lucid interval + temporal fracture. Not bridging veins (SDH, slower), not frontal contusions (don't progressively worsen), not berry aneurysm (no lucid interval).
A2A 72-year-old trips down the stairs without losing consciousness; 30 hours later she has headache and confusion and an occipital scalp bruise. Where is the bleed?
Subdural: bridging-vein tear in an atrophic brain; low-pressure venous bleeding → delayed, variable onset.
A3An 80-year-old nursing-home resident has 2 days of fluctuating consciousness, papilledema on the right and a right subdural collection. Vascular lesion?
Torn cerebral bridging veins, often after an unnoticed minor fall. (AVMs bleed into parenchyma in young adults; saccular aneurysms → basal SAH; MCA thrombosis → infarct.)
A4A 28-year-old with poorly controlled epilepsy is found dead on a pavement with blood from the back of his head. Autopsy shows an old plaque jaune, a scalp contusion, an occipital fracture and a large subdural hematoma. Immediate cause of death?
The large acute subdural hematoma (space-occupying bleed after a fall during a seizure). The plaque jaune is an old contusion from prior falls.

Q1. What does each type of force do to the brain?

CheckWhich mechanism best explains DAI without a skull fracture?
Inertial (rotational) loading: no contact needed; the brain twists inside the skull.

  • Scalp laceration/bruise = proof of contact injury. Occipital bruise → backward fall → look for contrecoup frontal/temporal contusions.
  • A scalp "goose egg" hematoma may crackle when pressed (fibrin), not because of broken bone.

Step 1 trap

An intact skull does not mean an intact brain: force is transmitted through the skull (contusions, DAI). Conversely a big skull fracture can sit over an uninjured brain.

Q3. Coup vs contrecoup: which side gets bruised?
  • Coup = contusion under the point of impact. Contrecoup = contusion on the opposite side, as the brain rebounds and strikes the inside of the skull.
  • Fall (moving head stops): brain keeps moving → contrecoup often worse than coup. Fall on the occiput → orbital frontal + temporal pole contusions.
  • Blow to a still head (punch, pipe): mainly coup; with more force some contrecoup appears, but the coup side still dominates.
  • Coup is essentially always present; contrecoup appears as severity rises.
CheckA man falls and strikes the left side of his head on the curb. Where is the worst contusion likely to be?
On the right (contrecoup), especially right frontal/temporal undersurface; a smaller left coup lesion too.
Memory tool

"Falls flip it, blows bruise it": a fall flips the damage to the opposite side (contrecoup); a blow bruises right under the hit (coup).

Step 1 HY

Backward fall (elderly, intoxicated) → orbitofrontal + anterior temporal contusions → disinhibition, anosmia (CN I shearing at the cribriform plate) and memory problems.

Q4. How is the brainstem or spinal cord injured?

Primary brainstem injury

Direct: e.g., hyperextension in a motorcycle crash → transection at the pontomedullary junction. Usually instantly fatal.

Secondary brainstem injury

Displacement from raised ICP → Duret hemorrhages, infarcts, tonsillar compression (LO 2).

  • Spinal cord injury: common (≈12–58 per million); causes = road traffic accidents, falls, sports, assaults.
  • Most are closed injuries with fracture or dislocation of the spine from hyperflexion / hyperextension.

Step 1 HY — spinal cord trauma

Elderly patient falls forward hitting the chin (hyperextension) → can't move hands well but walks → central cord syndrome. "Above T6, hypotension with bradycardia" → neurogenic shock (loss of sympathetic tone), not hemorrhagic shock (which gives tachycardia).

Q5. When should you suspect abusive (inflicted) head trauma in a child?
  • Head trauma is present in ≈60% of fatal child abuse; most abuse occurs under age 3.
  • Red flags: age <3, presentation in coma / low GCS, inconsistent or absent history, parental risk factors (young age, low socioeconomic status, education, family history).
  • Findings: subdural hematoma (bridging veins torn by acceleration–deceleration), retinal hemorrhages, DAI, contusion, cervical cord injury; skeletal survey may show posterior rib and metaphyseal ("corner") fractures.
  • Shaking alone more often injures the cervical cord; fatal inflicted brain trauma usually shows evidence of impact too. A controversial forensic area: always document carefully.
CheckInfant with SDH and bilateral retinal hemorrhages, parents report a fall from a couch. Next step?
The injuries don't fit a short fall → suspect abuse: admit for protection, skeletal survey, ophthalmology exam, notify child protective services.
Step 1 trap

"Shaken baby" bleeds are subdural (bridging veins), not epidural. Retinal hemorrhages + SDH + inconsistent history = abuse until proven otherwise; "clotting disorder" is a distractor unless labs say so.

Buzzwords
"raccoon eyes, Battle sign, rhinorrhea" → basilar fracture "hammer blow, fragments inward" → depressed fracture "fall on occiput" → frontal/temporal contrecoup "hyperextension, motorcycle" → pontomedullary transection "SDH + retinal hemorrhages, infant" → abusive head trauma "blast" → swelling; "penetrating" → local necrosis

1A 15-year-old is struck on the right side of the head by a baseball. He is unconscious for about a minute, then is alert and joking in the dugout. Forty-five minutes later he becomes drowsy, his right pupil is dilated and unreactive, and he has left-sided weakness. Skull radiograph shows a right temporal fracture. Injury to a vessel that enters the skull through which opening is most likely responsible?

C. Foramen spinosum. Lucid interval + temporal fracture = epidural hematoma from the middle meningeal artery (maxillary a. branch, enters via foramen spinosum, runs under the pterion). The blown right pupil is right uncal herniation; left weakness = compression of the right peduncle. Foramen ovale carries V3; jugular foramen carries the IJV (venous, not this tempo) and CN IX–XI; the carotid canal transmits the ICA (dissection, not EDH); vertebral arteries pass through the foramen magnum (basal SAH).

2A 68-year-old man with long-standing alcohol use disorder is brought in by his daughter for 3 weeks of worsening confusion, headache and unsteady gait. He remembers falling in his kitchen about a month ago but did not lose consciousness. Non-contrast CT shows a hypodense crescentic collection over the left convexity that crosses the coronal suture, with mild midline shift. Which vessel was most likely torn?

A. Crescent that crosses sutures, hypodense (old), weeks after a trivial fall in an atrophic brain = chronic subdural hematoma from bridging veins. MMA → biconvex EDH within hours; lenticulostriates → hypertensive putaminal ICH; ACom aneurysm → SAH with thunderclap headache; basilar perforators → Duret hemorrhages.

3A 50-year-old woman with a rapidly enlarging left temporal intracerebral hematoma becomes stuporous. Her left pupil is fixed and dilated. Unexpectedly, she has weakness of the LEFT arm and leg with a left Babinski sign. Which mechanism best explains the left-sided weakness?

D. Kernohan notch. The midbrain is shoved to the right, pressing the right peduncle against the tentorium → ipsilateral (left) hemiparesis, a false-localizing sign. Compressing the left peduncle (C) causes right weakness. Left ACA (B) → right leg. A temporal clot does not jump to the opposite capsule (A). Tonsillar herniation (E) → cardiorespiratory arrest, not hemiparesis.

4A 72-year-old man is found 90 minutes after onset of aphasia and right hemiplegia. Diffusion-weighted MRI shows a bright area in the left MCA territory involving cortex and underlying white matter; there is no contrast enhancement. The swelling at this stage is primarily the result of which process?

B. Cytotoxic edema. Early ischemia → ATP loss → pump failure → intracellular water; the barrier is still intact (no enhancement), grey and white matter both involved, DWI bright. (A) is vasogenic edema (days later). (C) is interstitial edema of hydrocephalus. (D) is congestion, not edema. (E) causes osmotic demyelination.

5A 45-year-old man with a large right frontal glioblastoma develops new weakness and numbness of the left foot and leg; the left arm and face are normal. Imaging shows midline shift with the right cingulate gyrus displaced across the midline. Compression of which structure explains the new deficit?

E. Subfalcine (cingulate) herniation pinches the ACA against the falx → infarct of the medial frontal/parietal cortex (leg area) → contralateral leg deficit. CN III and PCA are uncal herniation structures; the MCA supplies face and arm.

6A 24-year-old unrestrained driver is comatose at the scene of a high-speed rollover. There is no scalp laceration or skull fracture, and CT shows only a few tiny hemorrhages at the grey–white junction. He dies 3 hours later. Which finding in the corpus callosum would most likely confirm the diagnosis?

B. Diffuse axonal injury (rotational force, coma from the start, little on CT). At 3 hours only β-APP accumulation is detectable (<2 h with modern methods). Spheroids/microglial nodules (C) take ~24 h to weeks; red neurons (A) appear 12–24 h after hypoxia; hemosiderin (D) takes days; tau (E) is CTE.

7A 66-year-old woman slips on ice and falls backward, striking the back of her head. She has an occipital scalp hematoma. Where are cerebral contusions most likely to be found?

D. Contrecoup. In a fall, the moving brain rebounds onto the rough anterior and middle cranial fossae, bruising the orbital frontal gyri and temporal tips, usually worse than the small occipital coup lesion. (B) is the DAI distribution. Occipital-only (A) ignores contrecoup.

8A 30-year-old man is ejected from a car. He has bilateral periorbital ecchymoses, bruising behind the right ear, and clear fluid dripping from his nose that increases when he leans forward. For which complication is he at greatest risk over the coming days?

A. Raccoon eyes + Battle sign + CSF rhinorrhea = basilar skull fracture with a dural tear → open path from the sinuses to the subarachnoid space → meningitis (pneumococcus). EDH is tied to squamous temporal fractures with a lucid interval; CTE needs repeated injuries over years.

9A 22-year-old man is punched forcefully on the left side of the upper neck during a fight. He collapses within minutes and cannot be resuscitated. Autopsy shows thick subarachnoid blood covering the base of the brain and brainstem; the circle of Willis has no aneurysm, and the skull is intact. Which vessel was most likely injured?

E. Isolated massive basal SAH after a blow to the neck (not the head) in a young male = traumatic vertebral artery injury, with very rapid collapse. ACom = berry aneurysm (excluded). MMA → EDH; bridging veins → SDH; sinus → venous bleed/thrombosis.

10A patient with a large acute subdural hematoma becomes unresponsive. Blood pressure rises from 130/80 to 205/95 mm Hg, pulse falls from 88 to 46/min, and respirations become irregular. Which mechanism best explains the slow heart rate?

C. Cushing reflex. Raised ICP → brainstem ischemia → sympathetic surge to keep CPP (= MAP − ICP) up → hypertension → baroreceptors trigger vagal bradycardia; medullary compression → irregular breathing. (D) is neurogenic shock, which gives hypotension with bradycardia.

11A 58-year-old former professional football player has 10 years of depression, impulsivity and progressive memory loss, later with parkinsonism. At autopsy, which finding is most characteristic of his disease?

A. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy from repeated concussive/sub-concussive blows: extensive tau in neurons and glia (perivascular, sulcal depths), sometimes TDP-43. Lewy bodies = Parkinson disease; spongiform change = CJD; β-APP/spheroids = acute DAI; lacunes = hypertensive small-vessel disease.

12A 4-month-old boy is brought in for lethargy and vomiting. The father says the infant "rolled off the couch." There are no scalp bruises. CT shows bilateral subdural hematomas of different ages, and fundoscopy shows extensive bilateral retinal hemorrhages. Which mechanism most directly produced the intracranial bleeding?

D. Abusive head trauma. Subdural hematomas (bridging veins) + retinal hemorrhages + an inconsistent history in a child <3 → inflicted injury. No fracture argues against EDH; vitamin K deficiency presents earlier and with other bleeding; germinal matrix bleeds occur in premature neonates; berry aneurysms are rare in infants.

13A 38-year-old woman has sudden left-sided neck pain after a chiropractic neck adjustment. The next day she notices a droopy left eyelid; exam shows left miosis with normal facial sweating. Hours later she develops right hand weakness and difficulty finding words. What is the most likely diagnosis?

B. Neck pain + ipsilateral partial Horner (sympathetic plexus on the ICA; facial sweat fibers travel with the ECA) + contralateral cortical signs (aphasia, hand weakness) = carotid dissection with emboli to the left MCA. Vertebral dissection → posterior circulation signs (vertigo, dysphagia, crossed sensory loss). PCom aneurysm → CN III palsy with a dilated pupil. Pancoast → Horner with anhidrosis, no stroke.

14A 61-year-old man dies 2 days after a massive right hemispheric hemorrhage. At autopsy, the right uncus is grooved, and there are linear and flame-shaped hemorrhages in the midline of the midbrain and rostral pons. What caused the brainstem hemorrhages?

E. Duret hemorrhages. Transtentorial herniation pushes the brainstem down; the basilar artery is tethered above, so its paramedian perforators stretch and tear → midline midbrain/pontine bleeds (secondary brainstem injury). A hypertensive pontine bleed (A) is a primary event, not tied to herniation.
