MAPBig picture

  • Small molecules → dietary precursor → made in terminal (one rate-limiting enzyme) → vesicle → Ca²⁺ release → off by reuptake ± MAO / COMT / AChE
  • Fast wiring → glutamate (excite) · GABA / glycine (inhibit)
  • Tuning (neuromodulators) → amines · ACh · peptides · endocannabinoids → mostly GPCRs
  • One small nucleus → whole brain
    • Raphe → 5-HT · Locus coeruleus → NE · SNc / VTA → DA
    • Tuberomammillary → histamine · Basal nucleus of Meynert → ACh
  • Neuropeptides → made in soma → shipped down axon → high-frequency release → peptidases (no recycling)
  • Endocannabinoids → no vesicles → made on demand by postsynaptic cell → travel backward → quiet presynaptic terminal

LO 1Neuromodulators: the volume knobWhat they do and which transmitters can act this way

By the end: you can define volume transmission, explain how a neuromodulator changes another transmitter's effect, and list the seven neuromodulatory groups from memory.
Q1. A classic synapse is a private phone line. What is a neuromodulator?
  • A neuromodulator is a subset of neurotransmitters that can affect many neurons at once (whole groups), not just the one cell across the cleft.
  • It is released diffusely → volume transmission:
    • Release can happen away from the synapse (e.g., from varicosities along the axon).
    • Even if released at a synapse, it diffuses out of the cleft to neighboring cells.
  • Receptors: usually metabotropic (GPCRs) → longer-lasting effects; often located off to the side of the synapse or on the presynaptic terminal.
  • Effect: it does not necessarily carry excitation or inhibition itself — it changes how strongly other transmitters act (turns the volume up or down).
  • Classic example (presynaptic modulation): one terminal releases a neuropeptide from dense-core vesicles → it diffuses laterally to a neighboring terminal → binds presynaptic GPCRs → enhances release of that terminal's small-molecule transmitter.
CheckWhy do neuromodulator effects last longer than a glutamate EPSP?
They act mostly on metabotropic (G-protein) receptors, which work through second messengers (cAMP, kinases, channel phosphorylation) that persist for seconds to minutes, whereas ionotropic receptors close within milliseconds once the transmitter is cleared.
Q2. Which transmitters can act as neuromodulators?

Big groups

  • Neuropeptides
  • Lipid retrograde transmitters (endocannabinoids)

Amines

  • Dopamine
  • Norepinephrine
  • Serotonin
  • Histamine

Plus

  • Acetylcholine

NOT on the list: the fast amino acids glutamate, GABA, glycine.

  • Defining features of a neuromodulator:
    • Affects many neurons at the same time → diffuse release = volume transmission, away from the synapse.
    • Acts through metabotropic receptors → longer-lasting effect; often presynaptic.
    • Modifies the effect of other transmitters rather than carrying excitation/inhibition itself.
  • The seven neuromodulatory groups: neuropeptides → lipid retrograde transmitters → DA → NE → 5-HT → histamine → ACh.
CheckOf glutamate, histamine, glycine and enkephalin, which two are neuromodulators?
Histamine (amine) and enkephalin (neuropeptide). Glutamate and glycine are fast point-to-point amino acids.
Memory tool

"Everything except the three amino acids can modulate." Amino acids are the wires; everyone else is the dimmer switch.

Step 1 trap

ACh is a fast excitatory transmitter at the neuromuscular junction (nicotinic), but in the brain it mostly acts as a neuromodulator (basal forebrain → cortex, largely muscarinic). Same molecule, different job depending on receptor and location.

Step 1 HY

Almost every psychiatric drug targets a neuromodulator system (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, antipsychotics, stimulants, antihistamines, cholinesterase inhibitors), which is why their clinical effects take time and are "mood/arousal" rather than "on/off".

Buzzwords
"volume transmission" → neuromodulator"dense-core vesicles (dark on EM)" → neuropeptides"presynaptic modulation" → neuromodulator on neighbor terminal

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A neuropeptide released from one terminal increases glutamate release from an adjacent terminal it does not touch. Name the process and the receptor type most likely involved.
Volume transmission causing presynaptic modulation via a metabotropic (GPCR) receptor. The peptide diffuses laterally and tunes the neighbor's release instead of directly exciting a postsynaptic cell.
A2Predict: blocking a neuromodulator's receptor on its own changes resting membrane potential little, but the response to a glutamate input changes. Why?
Neuromodulators set gain, not signal. Their GPCR effects change channel properties and release probability, so the impact shows up as a different response to other transmitters.
A3On electron microscopy a terminal shows many small clear vesicles plus a few large dark vesicles off to the side. What does each hold?
Small clear = small-molecule transmitter (e.g., glutamate/GABA); large dense-core = neuropeptide (neuromodulator). Dense-core vesicles sit away from the active zone and need high-frequency firing to release.

"Biogenic amine" is the umbrella: catecholamines are biogenic amines too. If a question asks for "a biogenic amine that is not a catecholamine", the answer is serotonin or histamine.

Q2. What makes a transmitter "unconventional", and how are neuropeptides subdivided?

Unconventional transmitters

  • Not stored in vesicles; made on demand.
  • Often retrograde: made in the postsynaptic neuron, act on the presynaptic neuron.
  • Still count as transmitters because they carry signals between neurons and their release is Ca²⁺-dependent.
  • Example: endocannabinoids (AEA, 2-AG).

Neuropeptides: 5 loose groups (by amino acid sequence)

  • Brain/gut: substance P, CCK, VIP
  • Opioid: enkephalins, β-endorphin, dynorphins
  • Pituitary: vasopressin, oxytocin, ACTH
  • Hypothalamic releasing hormones: TRH, GnRH (LHRH), somatostatin
  • Miscellaneous: NPY, angiotensin II, neurotensin

The sequence determines biological activity, but you do not need to memorize sequences.

CheckWhy is THC not itself an endocannabinoid?
"Endo" means made by the body. THC (Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol) is an exogenous (plant) cannabinoid that mimics AEA/2-AG at CB1.
Step 1 HY

Orexin (hypocretin) is a hypothalamic neuropeptide that stabilizes wakefulness; loss of orexin neurons → narcolepsy with cataplexy (↓ CSF hypocretin-1). Suvorexant (orexin receptor antagonist) treats insomnia.

Buzzwords
"indoleamine" → serotonin"catechol ring" → DA, NE, Epi"retrograde, not stored in vesicles" → endocannabinoid"pre-propeptide" → neuropeptide

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A transmitter is detected in the cell body rough ER and in large dense-core vesicles transported down the axon. Which family?
Neuropeptide. Only peptides are made by ribosomes in the soma and shipped by axonal transport; small molecules are made in the terminal.
A2A molecule is made from membrane phospholipids in a dendritic spine after a Ca²⁺ rise and is never found inside vesicles. Classify it.
Unconventional lipid (retrograde) transmitter: an endocannabinoid (AEA or 2-AG).
A3Classify dynorphin, histamine, glycine and epinephrine.
Dynorphin = opioid neuropeptide; histamine = biogenic amine (not a catecholamine); glycine = amino acid; epinephrine = catecholamine.

  • AChE inhibitors for Alzheimer disease (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) boost ACh lost from the basal nucleus of Meynert; peripheral ones (pyridostigmine) for myasthenia gravis.

Q3. How is glutamate made, sensed and cleared?
  • Synthesis: glutamine → glutamate by mitochondrial glutaminase (rate-limiting in the summary table). Glutamate can also come from the TCA cycle (α-ketoglutarate).
  • Storage: VGLUT into small clear vesicles → Ca²⁺-dependent release.
  • Termination: excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs), mostly on astrocytes → astrocyte glutamine synthetase turns it into glutamine → glutamine returned to the neuron (glutamate–glutamine cycle).
  • Receptors:
    • Ionotropic (always excitatory): AMPA (Na⁺, fast EPSP), kainate (Na⁺), NMDA (Na⁺ and Ca²⁺; needs glycine co-agonist + depolarization to pop out the Mg²⁺ block).
    • Metabotropic: mGluR 1–8 (GPCRs).
CheckGlycine is an inhibitory transmitter, yet it is needed to open an excitatory receptor. Which one, and why is that not a contradiction?
The NMDA receptor. Glycine (or D-serine) binds a separate co-agonist site on NMDA; this has nothing to do with glycine's own Cl⁻ channel receptor in the spinal cord.
Step 1 HY — glutamate
  • Excitotoxicity (stroke, trauma): excess glutamate → NMDA Ca²⁺ overload → neuron death.
  • Memantine = NMDA antagonist for moderate–severe Alzheimer disease. Ketamine, PCP = NMDA antagonists (dissociation; PCP → psychosis, nystagmus, violence).
  • Riluzole (ALS) ↓ glutamate release.
Q4. How is GABA made and recycled?
  1. Precursor: glutamate, itself produced from glucose via the TCA cycle (and from glutamine).
  2. Glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) converts glutamate → GABA = rate-limiting; needs pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6). GAD is only in GABAergic neurons (a marker for them).
  3. Active transport into vesicles (VIAAT).
  4. Ca²⁺-triggered exocytosis; GABA can also diffuse away from the immediate postsynaptic site (reaching extrasynaptic receptors).
  5. Removal: reuptake by the GABA transporter (GAT) on astrocytes and presynaptic neurons.
  6. Astrocytes convert GABA → glutamine, which is released and taken up by neurons to make glutamate → GABA again = GABA/glutamine cycle.
CheckWhy would vitamin B6 deficiency cause seizures?
GAD needs pyridoxal phosphate (B6). Low B6 → ↓GAD activity → ↓GABA → loss of inhibition → seizures.
Step 1 HY — B6 and seizures

Isoniazid causes B6 deficiency → ↓GABA → seizures (also sideroblastic anemia, peripheral neuropathy) → give pyridoxine. Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy of infancy: seizures resistant to standard drugs, stopped by IV B6. B6 is also the cofactor for AADC (DOPA → DA, 5-HTP → 5-HT) and histidine decarboxylase.

Step 1 trap

The astrocyte returns glutamine, not glutamate and not GABA. Glutamine is non-neuroactive, so it can be shuttled safely through extracellular space.

Q5. What are the two GABA receptors and which drugs act on them?

GABA-A · ionotropic

  • Pentameric ligand-gated anion channel (mainly Cl⁻) → Cl⁻ influx → hyperpolarization (fast).
  • 2 GABA binding sites (at α/β interfaces).
  • Classic agonist: muscimol · classic antagonist: bicuculline · channel blocker: picrotoxin (→ seizures).
  • Potentiated (separate sites) by benzodiazepines, ethanol, barbiturates, neurosteroids.

GABA-B · metabotropic

  • GPCR → opens K⁺ channels (K⁺ efflux) and inhibits Ca²⁺ influx.
  • Slow inhibition.
  • Widely distributed; implicated in seizures, dystonia, pain, psychiatric disorders, autoimmune limbic encephalitis.

Enhance GABA → CNS depressants

  • Barbiturates
  • Alcohol
  • Anesthetics
  • Benzodiazepines
  • Zolpidem (Ambien)
  • Muscimol

Block GABA → seizures

  • Picrotoxin (channel blocker)
  • Bicuculline (competitive antagonist)
  • Where GABA receptors sit: neuron cell body (quick shut-off of AP firing), dendritic shaft, extrasynaptic sites, and presynaptic autoreceptors.
  • Overall effect of GABA: transient inhibition of AP firing. Fun fact: GABA is excitatory in the developing brain (immature neurons have high intracellular Cl⁻, so opening Cl⁻ channels depolarizes).
CheckPicrotoxin is applied to cortical tissue. Predict the activity and name another drug with the same effect.
Blocking the GABA-A channel removes inhibition → seizure-like activity. Bicuculline does the same by competing with GABA.
Step 1 HY — GABA pharmacology
  • Benzodiazepines ↑ frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening (need GABA; site at α/γ interface); reversed by flumazenil.
  • Barbiturates ↑ duration of opening (can open the channel directly at high dose → more lethal; contraindicated in porphyria).
  • Baclofen = GABA-B agonist for spasticity. Vigabatrin inhibits GABA transaminase; tiagabine blocks GAT-1.
  • Alcohol/benzo withdrawal → ↓GABA tone → seizures, DTs → treat with long-acting benzodiazepine.
Memory tool

"Frenzodiazepines ↑ FREquency, Barbidurates ↑ DURation." And B for Baclofen, B for GABA-B.

Step 1 trap

Zolpidem is not a benzodiazepine structurally but acts at the benzodiazepine site (BZ1/α1) and is also reversed by flumazenil.

Q6. How does glycine work, and why does blocking it cause convulsions?
  1. Precursor: serine → glycine by serine hydroxymethyltransferase (rate-limiting).
  2. Loaded into vesicles by the same transporter used by GABA (VIAAT).
  3. Ca²⁺-triggered exocytosis.
  4. Reuptake by glycine transporters on astrocytes and presynaptic neurons; astrocytes turn glycine back into serine, which is released for neuronal reuptake.
  • Receptors: ionotropic ONLY: several subtypes of glycine-gated Cl⁻ channels, pentamers like GABA-A → Cl⁻ in → inhibition.
  • Location: brainstem and spinal cord; about ½ of spinal inhibitory synapses use glycine.
  • Strychnine blocks the glycine receptor (closes the pore) → loss of inhibition → convulsions.
  • Glycine is also a co-agonist at NMDA receptors.
  • Clinical: mutations in glycine transporter or degradation-enzyme genes → hyperglycinemia, a devastating neonatal disease (lethargy, seizures, cognitive deficits).
CheckA child eats rat poison and develops muscle spasms and opisthotonus while fully awake. Which receptor is blocked?
Glycine receptor (strychnine). Spinal/brainstem disinhibition → spasms; the cortex is spared, so the patient stays conscious.
Step 1 HY — glycine
  • Tetanus toxin travels retrogradely to the spinal cord and cleaves synaptobrevin in Renshaw cells → blocks release of glycine and GABA → spastic paralysis, trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonus. Same picture as strychnine but by blocking release, not the receptor.
  • Boards call the neonatal disease nonketotic hyperglycinemia (glycine cleavage system defect): ↑CSF:plasma glycine ratio, hiccups, seizures, apnea.

Q7. How are the catecholamines (DA → NE → Epi) built?
  1. Tyrosine → L-DOPA by tyrosine hydroxylase (rate-limiting, cytoplasm).
  2. L-DOPA → dopamine by DOPA decarboxylase = aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC).
  3. Dopamine pumped into vesicles by VMAT.
  4. Inside the vesicle, dopamine β-hydroxylase (DBH) makes NE. DBH identifies NE neurons. NE is the only transmitter whose last synthetic step happens inside the vesicle.
  5. In epinephrine neurons (and adrenal medulla), NE leaks out to the cytoplasm → PNMT → epinephrine → transported back into vesicles.
CheckA neuron contains tyrosine hydroxylase and AADC but no DBH. What does it release?
Dopamine. Without DBH the chain stops at dopamine.
Step 1 HY — catecholamine synthesis
  • PKU: ↓phenylalanine hydroxylase (or ↓BH4) → tyrosine becomes essential, ↓catecholamines/melanin → intellectual disability, musty odor, fair skin. BH4 deficiency also ↓tyrosine and tryptophan hydroxylase → ↓DA and ↓5-HT.
  • Levodopa + carbidopa (Parkinson): L-DOPA crosses the BBB (dopamine does not); carbidopa blocks peripheral AADC → more L-DOPA reaches brain, less nausea/arrhythmia.
  • Cortisol induces PNMT → adrenal medulla (bathed in cortical blood) makes mostly epinephrine.
  • DBH needs vitamin C (and copper); Menkes (ATP7A, Cu transport) → ↓DBH activity.
  • Reserpine / tetrabenazine block VMAT → deplete monoamines (tetrabenazine for Huntington chorea).
Memory tool

Cofactor ladder "B-H-4, B-6, C, SAM": Tyr hydroxylase = BH4 → AADC = B6 → DBH = vit C → PNMT = SAM (methyl donor; PNMT puts the Methyl on).

Q8. How are catecholamines switched off, and what receptors do they use?

Termination

  • Reuptake first: DAT (dopamine), NET (NE) on the presynaptic terminal → reloaded into vesicles (VMAT) or degraded.
  • MAO (on the outer mitochondrial membrane) in the terminal: DA → DOPAC.
  • COMT (cytoplasm, mostly outside the terminal: glia, postsynaptic cells, liver): → DA becomes homovanillic acid (HVA).
  • NE/Epi end products: VMA (and metanephrines, MHPG in brain).

Receptors: metabotropic ONLY

  • D1-like (D1, D5): Gs → ↑adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → ↑protein phosphorylation (stimulatory).
  • D2-like (D2, D3, D4): Gi → ↓AC → ↓cAMP → ↓phosphorylation (inhibitory).
  • NE, Epi → α and β adrenergic receptors, coupled to stimulatory or inhibitory G proteins.
  • Presynaptic adrenergic receptors = inhibitory autoreceptors (Gi).

CheckDopamine excites some striatal neurons and inhibits others. How?
Cells with D1 (Gs) are stimulated (↑cAMP); cells with D2 (Gi) are inhibited (↓cAMP). Same transmitter, different receptor.
Step 1 HY — urine metabolites
  • HVA = dopamine breakdown; VMA + metanephrines = NE/Epi breakdown; 5-HIAA = serotonin breakdown.
  • Neuroblastoma (child, abdominal mass, opsoclonus-myoclonus) → ↑urine HVA and VMA. Pheochromocytoma → ↑ metanephrines, VMA; episodic HTN, headache, sweating. Carcinoid syndrome → ↑ 5-HIAA.
  • Boards detail: α1 = Gq, α2 = Gi (presynaptic autoreceptor; clonidine), β1/β2 = Gs.
Step 1 trap

Only MAO sits on mitochondria; COMT is cytosolic and largely extraneuronal. Also: reuptake, not enzymatic breakdown, is the main way catecholamine action ends at the synapse (contrast ACh, where the enzyme AChE ends it).

Q9. How is serotonin (5-HT) made, used and cleared?
  1. Precursor: tryptophan → 5-hydroxytryptophan by tryptophan-5-hydroxylase (rate-limiting).
  2. 5-HTP → serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC).
  3. VMAT loads vesicles → Ca²⁺ exocytosis → diffuses across cleft to receptors.
  4. Removed by the specific presynaptic transporter SERT → metabolized by MAO or reloaded into vesicles.

5-HT3 · ionotropic (the only one)

  • Non-selective cation channel → excitatory postsynaptic responses.
  • Blockers treat postoperative and chemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting (ondansetron).

All other 5-HT receptors · metabotropic (most)

  • Control circadian rhythm, motor behavior, emotional state, arousal, satiety/food intake.
  • Dysfunction → depression, anxiety disorders, schizophrenia.
  • LSD activates metabotropic 5-HT receptors → hallucinations.
CheckWhich serotonin receptor would you block to stop chemotherapy vomiting, and what kind of receptor is it?
5-HT3, a ligand-gated non-selective cation channel (the only ionotropic 5-HT receptor). Drug: ondansetron.
Step 1 HY — serotonin
  • SSRIs block SERT; SNRIs block SERT + NET; MAOIs block breakdown.
  • Serotonin syndrome: SSRI/SNRI + MAOI, linezolid, tramadol, meperidine, triptans, dextromethorphan, St John's wort → hyperthermia, confusion, autonomic instability, clonus/hyperreflexia, diarrhea within hours → treat with cyproheptadine (5-HT2 antagonist).
  • Ondansetron (5-HT3 antagonist): QT prolongation, constipation, headache.
  • LSD = 5-HT2A agonist. Carcinoid: tumor diverts tryptophan → ↑5-HIAA and niacin deficiency (pellagra).
Step 1 trap — serotonin syndrome vs NMS

Both: fever, altered mental status, autonomic instability. Serotonin syndrome = fast (hours), clonus, hyperreflexia, diarrhea, after a serotonergic combo. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome = slower (days), lead-pipe rigidity, ↓reflexes, ↑CK, after a D2 blocker (treat dantrolene/bromocriptine).

Q10. How does histamine work in the brain?
  1. Histidine → histamine by histidine decarboxylase (rate-limiting).
  2. VMAT loads vesicles → Ca²⁺ exocytosis.
  3. Metabolized by MAO and histamine methyltransferase.
  • Receptors: four types (H1–H4), all metabotropic.
  • CNS histamine neurons are in the hypothalamus (tuberomammillary nucleus) → sparse but widespread projections to almost all brain areas and the spinal cord.
  • Functions: arousal and attention; controls reactivity of the vestibular system.
  • Peripheral histamine from mast cells → allergic responses. Antihistamines that cross the BBB (diphenhydramine, Benadryl) are sedatives (↓arousal).
CheckWhy does diphenhydramine help motion sickness and cause drowsiness, while fexofenadine does neither?
Diphenhydramine crosses the BBB and blocks central H1 receptors that drive arousal and vestibular reactivity; fexofenadine (2nd generation) barely enters the brain.
Step 1 trap

Histamine has no specific reuptake transporter in the in-house scheme; it is ended by enzymes (MAO + histamine N-methyltransferase). Also, it is not a catecholamine even though it shares VMAT and MAO.

Step 1 HY

1st-generation H1 blockers (diphenhydramine, doxylamine, meclizine, promethazine) → sedation + anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, urinary retention, delirium in elderly). Modafinil and pitolisant (H3 inverse agonist) promote wakefulness partly via histamine.

Q11. How do neuropeptides and opioids work?
  • Synthesis: in the cell body as large pre-propeptides (RER → Golgi) → packaged into dense-core vesicles → axonal transport to the terminal.
  • Processing: signal sequence cleaved → proteolytic cleavage can yield several active peptides from one precursor (diversity); further processing can even occur after release.
  • Release: Ca²⁺-dependent, but needs high-frequency firing (see LO 6).
  • Receptors: GPCRs only, very high affinity → act at low concentrations; synaptic and extrasynaptic → modulatory.
  • Termination: extracellular endo- and exopeptidases; renewal requires new synthesis in the soma.
  • Roles: NPY → starting and maintaining feeding (satiety/obesity); peptides also in anxiety/panic, analgesia, and regulating sympathetic ganglion output and gut activity.
  • Neuropeptides can be co-localized with small-molecule transmitters in the same terminal (e.g., opioids with GABA).

Q12. How do endocannabinoids signal "backwards"?
  • What they are: anandamide (AEA) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG), unsaturated fatty acids with polar head groups.
  • Made on demand by enzymatic breakdown of membrane lipids in the postsynaptic neuron when Ca²⁺ activates a second-messenger cascade.
  • Released by simply diffusing through the membrane (not stored in vesicles, yet release is still Ca²⁺-dependent) → bind presynaptic CB1 receptors = retrograde signaling.
  • Terminated by hydrolysis: fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH).
  • Receptors: metabotropic only, Gi-coupled → inhibitory on the target (presynaptic) neuron → transient or long-lasting ↓ transmitter release.
  • Affect appetite, pain sensation, mood and memory.
  • Exogenous cannabinoid: Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) from Cannabis.

CB1

  • On neurons; the dominant brain receptor.
  • Enriched in substantia nigra and caudate-putamen (areas implicated in drug abuse); also neocortex, cerebellum, hippocampus, hypothalamus, spinal cord.
  • Mainly on inhibitory (GABAergic) terminals.

CB2

  • Immune cells, widespread in the periphery.
  • Also on microglia and astrocytes; function unclear.
  • Much lower levels than CB1 in brain.
CheckCB1 activation inhibits a terminal. Why can the postsynaptic neuron end up more excited?
CB1 is mostly on GABAergic terminals. Less GABA released → less inhibition of the postsynaptic cell → disinhibition (net excitation).
Step 1 HY — cannabinoids
  • Cannabis intoxication: euphoria, conjunctival injection, ↑appetite, dry mouth, tachycardia, slowed time perception, paranoia.
  • Dronabinol (synthetic THC): chemotherapy nausea, AIDS anorexia. Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: cyclic vomiting relieved by hot showers.
  • Boards name both off-switches: FAAH (AEA) and monoacylglycerol lipase, MAGL (2-AG).
Step 1 trap

AEA and 2-AG are the endocannabinoids (they are not precursors of something else). And THC is an exogenous phytocannabinoid, not an "exogenous endocannabinoid".

A1A man on phenelzine eats aged cheddar and drinks red wine, then develops a pounding headache and BP 220/130. Which enzyme is inhibited and why does this happen?
Monoamine oxidase. Dietary tyramine normally gets destroyed by gut/liver MAO; with MAO blocked, tyramine enters nerve terminals and displaces stored NE → massive NE release → hypertensive crisis.

Enkephalin. Peptides are destroyed by extracellular peptidases and must be resynthesized in the soma and transported down the axon; GABA is taken back up and remade locally in the terminal.
A3A patient with Parkinson disease is started on levodopa alone and gets severe nausea with little benefit. What should be added and how does it work?

A5A drug increases the frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening only when GABA is present. Name the class, the receptor and the antidote.
Benzodiazepine, acting at the α/γ site of the GABA-A receptor; reversed by flumazenil.

  • The four core differences (small molecule vs neuropeptide):
    1. Site of synthesis and packaging into vesicles (terminal vs soma).
    2. Diffusion (nanometers vs micrometers).
    3. Termination mechanism (reuptake/enzymes vs peptidases).

CheckAt low firing rates, a terminal containing glutamate and NPY releases only glutamate. Why?
Clear glutamate vesicles at the active zone fuse with the brief local Ca²⁺ spike of single APs; NPY dense-core vesicles sit laterally and need the diffuse, sustained Ca²⁺ rise produced by high-frequency trains.
Memory tool

"SPDT" → "Some Peptides Don't Turn over": Site (soma), Packaging (dense-core), Diffusion (wide), Termination (peptidases), and they need a strong Train to be released. Small molecules = local factory with recycling; peptides = central factory with one-way shipping.

Step 1 trap

Catecholamines can also be stored in dense-core vesicles (small or large), so "dense-core" alone does not prove "peptide". The decisive peptide clues are soma synthesis + axonal transport and no reuptake.

ApplyLO 6 application questions

A1A toxin blocks fast axonal transport. Which transmitter supply fails first at the terminal: glutamate or substance P?
Substance P. Peptides (and their vesicles) must be shipped from the soma; glutamate is made and recycled locally from glutamine.
A2A peptidase inhibitor is applied to the spinal dorsal horn. Predict the effect on enkephalin signaling and pain.
Enkephalins persist longer (their only off-switch is extracellular peptidases) → ↑ endogenous analgesia.
A3Why do neuropeptides affect neurons several micrometers away while GABA mostly does not?
Peptides are released away from the active zone, are not rapidly taken back up, and their GPCRs have very high affinity, so even dilute peptide activates distant extrasynaptic receptors. GABA is cleared within milliseconds by GAT and needs high local concentrations.

1A 34-year-old man started on treatment for latent tuberculosis 3 months ago is brought to the emergency department after a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. He has no history of epilepsy. He also reports numbness of both feet. Reduced activity of which enzyme most directly explains the seizure?

C. Glutamic acid decarboxylase. Isoniazid depletes pyridoxal phosphate (B6), the cofactor for GAD, the rate-limiting enzyme of GABA synthesis → ↓GABA → seizures (neuropathy is also from B6 loss). Tyrosine hydroxylase uses BH4, not B6. ↓glutaminase would lower glutamate (less excitation). AChE inhibition causes cholinergic crisis, not isolated seizure with neuropathy. SHMT makes glycine and is not B6-limited in this scenario.

2A 4-year-old boy is brought in after eating pellets from a garage shelf. He has painful generalized muscle spasms triggered by noise, arching of the back and trismus, but is alert and responds appropriately between spasms. The toxin most likely acts by which mechanism?

A. Strychnine (rat poison) blocks the glycine receptor, a pentameric Cl⁻ channel concentrated in the brainstem and spinal cord (glycine is ~half of spinal inhibitory synapses). Loss of inhibition → spasms with preserved consciousness. Botulinum (B) causes flaccid paralysis. Organophosphates (C) cause cholinergic crisis with miosis and secretions. GABA-B blockade (D) is not the mechanism of any classic poison. EAAT inhibition (E) would cause excitotoxicity but not this toxidrome.

3A 52-year-old woman treated for refractory depression with an older antidepressant attends a wine-tasting party. Within an hour she develops a severe occipital headache, palpitations and BP of 210/124 mm Hg. Inhibition of which enzyme is responsible?

D. MAO. MAOIs prevent breakdown of dietary tyramine (aged cheese, wine, cured meats), which displaces stored NE → hypertensive crisis. COMT inhibitors (entacapone) are used in Parkinson and do not cause this reaction. DBH, tyrosine hydroxylase and PNMT inhibition would decrease catecholamines.

4A 66-year-old man with resting tremor and bradykinesia is prescribed levodopa combined with a second drug that does not cross the blood-brain barrier. The second drug allows a lower levodopa dose and reduces nausea. What is its target?

B. Carbidopa inhibits peripheral AADC (DOPA decarboxylase), preventing conversion of L-DOPA to dopamine outside the brain (dopamine cannot cross the BBB and causes nausea/arrhythmias). Selegiline (A) acts centrally on MAO-B. Entacapone (C) inhibits COMT, also used but it is not the classic partner that reduces nausea. Inhibiting tyrosine hydroxylase (D) or VMAT (E) would worsen parkinsonism.

5A researcher records from a small cluster of pigmented neurons in the dorsal pons beneath the floor of the fourth ventricle. Firing rises sharply when the animal is startled or stressed and falls almost to zero in REM sleep. These neurons release which transmitter?

E. Norepinephrine from the locus coeruleus, which regulates sleep-wake cycles and responses to fear and stress. Raphe (A) neurons are midline and serotonergic. Pigmented dopamine neurons (B) are in the midbrain SNc. Cholinergic basal forebrain (C) and histaminergic hypothalamic neurons (D) are rostral.

6A 30-year-old woman takes an over-the-counter antihistamine for seasonal allergies and becomes very drowsy at work. A colleague's newer antihistamine does not cause sleepiness. The sedation from the first drug is best explained by blockade of receptors whose transmitter originates in which structure?

C. Hypothalamic histamine neurons project widely and mediate arousal and attention through metabotropic H1 receptors. First-generation antihistamines (diphenhydramine) cross the BBB; second-generation agents do not. The other nuclei are sources of 5-HT, NE, DA and ACh.

7A patient receiving cisplatin is given ondansetron for vomiting. The receptor targeted by this drug differs from all other receptors for the same transmitter in which way?

A. 5-HT3 is the only ionotropic serotonin receptor (non-selective cation channel, excitatory); its blockers treat chemotherapy-induced and postoperative nausea. All other 5-HT receptors are GPCRs (B is a metabotropic feature). Cl⁻ channels (C) are GABA-A and glycine. LSD (E) acts on metabotropic 5-HT2A receptors.

8In hippocampal tissue, strong depolarization of a pyramidal neuron transiently reduces the inhibitory postsynaptic currents it receives. The effect is abolished by a CB1 antagonist. Which statement best describes the messenger?

D. Endocannabinoid retrograde signaling (depolarization-induced suppression of inhibition): Ca²⁺ in the postsynaptic cell triggers AEA/2-AG production from membrane lipids; they diffuse back to presynaptic CB1 (Gi) on GABAergic terminals → ↓GABA release. They are not stored in vesicles (A), act retrogradely (not B), use metabotropic receptors only (not C) and are hydrolyzed by FAAH (not E).

9A hypothalamic neuron contains both glutamate and neuropeptide Y. At a firing rate of 2 Hz, only glutamate is detected in the extracellular space; at 40 Hz, both are detected. What best explains this?

B. Neuropeptides need high-frequency trains of APs that produce a diffuse Ca²⁺ rise reaching laterally placed dense-core vesicles. Peptides are made in the soma, not the terminal (A); release is Ca²⁺-dependent (C); they are removed by extracellular peptidases, not reuptake (D); retrograde signaling describes endocannabinoids (E).

10A 58-year-old man has episodic facial flushing, watery diarrhea and wheezing. CT shows multiple liver masses. He also has a scaly rash on sun-exposed skin. Which urine test best supports the diagnosis?

E. 5-HIAA, the MAO metabolite of serotonin, is elevated in carcinoid syndrome (liver metastases let 5-HT bypass hepatic MAO). The tumor diverts tryptophan away from niacin → pellagra-like rash. HVA (A) reflects dopamine (neuroblastoma); VMA (B) and metanephrines (C) reflect NE/Epi (pheochromocytoma, neuroblastoma); methylhistamine (D) suggests mastocytosis.

11A 76-year-old woman has 3 years of progressive short-term memory loss, getting lost in familiar places and difficulty with finances. Her physician starts donepezil. Degeneration of which structure underlies the transmitter deficit this drug addresses?

C. Alzheimer disease → loss of cholinergic neurons in the basal nucleus of Meynert → ↓cortical ACh; donepezil inhibits AChE. SNc (A) = Parkinson (↓DA). Caudate (D) atrophy = Huntington (↓GABA, ↓ACh). Locus coeruleus (B) and raphe (E) are NE and 5-HT sources.

12Bath-applying transmitter X to a neuron produces no response for ~200 ms, then a slow hyperpolarization lasting several seconds. The response is abolished by pertussis toxin, and no ionotropic receptor for X has ever been found. X is most likely which of the following?
