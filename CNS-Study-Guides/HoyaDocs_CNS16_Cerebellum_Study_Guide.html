Cerebellum & Cerebellar Disorders

Learning objectives

  1. LO 1Find the cerebellum's lobes, fissures, longitudinal zones and peduncles on a specimen, and pair each of the four deep nuclei with the strip of cortex that feeds it.
  2. LO 2Name the three layers of cerebellar cortex and the five neuron types that live in them, including which ones excite and which inhibit.
  3. LO 3Sort the cerebellum's inputs by where they start (spinal cord, brainstem, cerebral cortex) and say what information each one delivers.
  4. LO 4List the cerebellum’s major exit routes and trace its two big circuits (the spinal loop and the cerebral loop) step by step: Purkinje cell → deep nucleus → brainstem, cord or motor-cortex target.
  5. LO 5Use the two midline crossings (cerebellum → thalamus → cortex, then corticospinal) to explain why cerebellar signs appear on the same side as the lesion.
  6. LO 6Predict what anterior-, posterior- and flocculonodular-lobe lesions look like at the bedside, and use that to localize the lesion in the "failed sobriety test" case.

MAPBig picture

  • ~10% of brain volume → >50% of its neurons (mostly granule cells)
  • Cortex decides what → cerebellum decides how (how far, how fast, how smooth, when to stop)
  • Compares plan (cortex via pons) vs actual movement (cord, vestibular, olive) → corrects now (feedback) → predicts next time (feedforward)
  • Inputs → cortex → Purkinje (−) → deep nuclei (+) → brainstem / thalamus
    • Vermis → fastigial · paravermis → interposed · lateral hemisphere → dentate
    • Flocculonodular lobe → vestibular nuclei
  • Output crosses → descending tracts cross back → ipsilateral signs
  • Lesion → no paralysis, no numbness → missed targets · sway · scanning speech (looks drunk)

LO 1Gross anatomy & deep nucleiLobes, fissures, zones, peduncles, and which strip of cortex feeds which nucleus

By the end: you can label lobes, fissures, vermis/paravermis/hemisphere and the three peduncles on a specimen, list the deep nuclei lateral → medial, and match each to its cortical zone and functional division.
Q1. Where is the cerebellum, and what is it made of?
  • Sits in the posterior cranial fossa, dorsal to the pons and medulla, and forms the roof (posterior wall) of the 4th ventricle.
  • Like the cerebrum: outer gray matter cortex folded into thin leaves (folia, the "branches") over an inner white matter core (the "trunk"; together = arbor vitae, "tree of life").
  • Buried in that white matter: the deep cerebellar nuclei, just above the roof of the 4th ventricle.
  • Attached to the brainstem by three pairs of peduncles (superior, middle, inferior).
CheckWhy might a swollen cerebellum (hemorrhage, big tumor) cause hydrocephalus?
It forms the roof of the 4th ventricle; mass effect compresses the ventricle/aqueduct outflow → obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus. It can also compress the brainstem, a neurosurgical emergency.
Q2. How is it divided into lobes (the transverse cuts)?
LobeBoundaryBody map / role
AnteriorAbove the primary fissureTrunk + proximal limbs → posture, gait, tone
PosteriorBetween primary and posterolateral fissures (largest lobe)Bigger map of distal limbs → fine, skilled coordination; also cognition
FlocculonodularBelow the posterolateral fissure: nodulus (midline) + 2 flocculiBalance + eye movements (vestibular)
  • Anterior + posterior = corpus cerebelli.
  • Horizontal fissure: within the posterior lobe, separates superior from inferior surface.
  • Tonsils: inferior, medial parts of the posterior lobe, right above the foramen magnum.
CheckWhich fissure isolates the "balance" part of the cerebellum, and which part of it lies in the midline?
The posterolateral fissure separates the flocculonodular lobe; the nodulus is its midline (vermal) part, the two flocculi are lateral.
Q3. How is it divided from medial to lateral (the longitudinal strips), and which deep nucleus serves each strip?

This is the organizing principle of the whole topic: medial cortex → medial nucleus → medial (axial) motor systems; lateral cortex → lateral nucleus → limb and planning systems.

Cortical zoneFunctional divisionDeep nucleusControls
Lateral hemisphereCerebrocerebellumDentatePlanning, timing, sequencing of skilled distal movement; cognition

  • All four deep nuclei sit in the white matter; dentate = largest, most lateral.
  • The deep nuclei are the only true efferent pathway out of the cerebellum (flocculonodular Purkinje cells reach the vestibular nuclei directly) → lesion them and the cortex cannot "talk" to the rest of the brain.
CheckPurkinje cells in the paravermis are damaged. Which deep nucleus loses its inhibitory input?
The interposed nuclei (emboliform + globose), which drive the red nucleus/rubrospinal system for distal limb control.
Memory tool — deep nuclei

"Don't Eat Greasy Food" = Dentate, Emboliform, Globose, Fastigial, from lateral → medial. Fastigial is closest to the Fourth ventricle roof (midline). E + G = "interposed" because they are placed between dentate and fastigial.

Step 1 trap

The dentate nucleus (cerebellum) and the inferior olivary nucleus (medulla) both look like a crumpled, wavy bag of gray matter on sections. Check the location: olive = ventral medulla beside the pyramid; dentate = cerebellar white matter.

Memory tool — peduncles

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A neurosurgeon reports a tumor "arising from the nodulus." Which functional division and deep-nucleus equivalent are involved, and what signs do you expect?
Vestibulocerebellum; output via the vestibular nuclei. Expect nystagmus, vertigo, truncal imbalance with a tendency to fall, but little limb dysmetria.
A2A lesion confined to the most medial deep nucleus would mostly affect which muscles?
Axial and proximal muscles (trunk, posture, gait). The fastigial nucleus is the vermis's output to vestibulospinal and reticulospinal systems.
A3On a horizontal section you see a large, wavy gray band lateral in the cerebellar white matter. Name it and its cortical input.
Dentate nucleus, receiving Purkinje axons from the lateral hemisphere (cerebrocerebellum).
A4Why can a small stroke cause only truncal signs, or only nystagmus, or only one-limb dysmetria?
The vermis, flocculonodular lobe and lateral hemispheres have separate arterial territories (and separate functions), so a stroke can hit one zone in isolation.

LO 2Cerebellar cortex: layers & neuronsThree layers, five cell types, one output

By the end: you can name the layers from surface to depth, place each of the five neurons, give its sign (+/−), and explain why the Purkinje cell is the bottleneck of the whole system.
Q1. What are the three layers of cerebellar cortex?
Layer (surface → deep)Contents
1. MolecularStellate (−) and basket (−) cells; Purkinje dendrites; parallel fibers. Few cells → pale pink.
2. PurkinjeOne row of large Purkinje cell bodies (−)
3. GranularHuge numbers of granule cells (+) and Golgi cells (−); mossy fibers end here. Dark blue (tiny nuclei).

Below the granular layer is white matter, then the deep nuclei.

CheckWhich layer would you see as a dense sea of tiny dark nuclei on H&E?
The granular layer. Granule cells are the most numerous neurons in the brain, which is why the cerebellum holds >50% of all neurons.
Memory tool — layers

Surface to depth: "MPG" — Molecular, Purkinje, Granular (like miles per gallon). Neurons by layer: "Stella and her Basket in the Molecule; Golgi and the Granules down below."

Q2. What are the five neurons, and which ones excite?
NeuronLayerSignJob

CheckOf the five cortical neuron types, how many are inhibitory?
Four (Golgi, Purkinje, stellate, basket). Only the granule cell is excitatory.

Step 1 HY
  • Purkinje cells: GABAergic, inhibitory, sole output of the cerebellar cortex, target the deep nuclei (and vestibular nuclei).
  • Deep nuclei: excitatory output to the brainstem and thalamus.
  • Alcohol potentiates GABA-A transmission and hits the cerebellum first → ataxia, slurred speech, dysmetria, nystagmus (the roadside sobriety test is a cerebellar exam). Chronic use → anterior vermis degeneration (wide-based gait, arms relatively spared).
Step 1 trap

Mossy fibers do NOT synapse on Purkinje cells. They synapse on granule cells, whose parallel fibers then contact Purkinje dendrites. Only climbing fibers synapse directly on Purkinje cells. Also: the Purkinje cell is inhibitory, but the cerebellum's final output (deep nuclei) is excitatory.

Buzzwords
"only excitatory cortical neuron" → granule cell"sole output of cerebellar cortex" → Purkinje cell (GABA)"largest neurons in the CNS" → Purkinje cells"parallel fibers" → granule cell axons

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A drug selectively blocks GABA-A receptors on deep-nucleus neurons. Which synapse is affected?
The Purkinje → deep nucleus synapse. It is the only GABAergic input arriving at the deep nuclei from the cortex.
A2After several drinks, a man has a wide-based gait, slurred speech and misses his nose. Explain at the synaptic level.
Ethanol enhances GABA-A (inhibitory) transmission in the cerebellum, transiently disrupting the timing of cerebellar output and mimicking cerebellar disease.
A3A pathologist sees loss of the large neurons forming a single row between the pale and the dark layers. Which cells, and what is lost functionally?
Purkinje cells (seen in alcoholic degeneration, paraneoplastic anti-Yo, some SCAs). The cortex loses its only output, so the deep nuclei run without correction → ataxia.

LO 3Afferents: what goes inSpinal cord, brainstem and cerebral cortex inputs, and what each carries

By the end: you can list each input pathway by origin, give the peduncle it uses and its fiber type (mossy vs climbing), and say what information it brings.
Q1. What information does the cerebellum need, and why?

Its job is to blend the cortical plan with constantly changing information and execute it fast. To compare plan vs reality it needs:

Interoceptive (inside the body)

  • Body position (non-conscious proprioception: spindles, GTOs)
  • Head position (vestibular)
  • Muscle tone

Exteroceptive (outside world)

  • Vision
  • Hearing
  • Touch

Plus the plan itself from the cerebral cortex. The cerebellum's own functions: timing of movement, maintaining posture/position in space, maintaining muscle tone, and timing and sequencing of language, attention and working memory.

CheckReaching for a cup requires five things. Name them.
Contract the right muscles; relax the antagonists; predict where the hand/mouth will end up; time each muscle group precisely; and string it into a sequence (reach, grip, lift, carry, aim, release).

The granular layer: pontocerebellar axons are mossy fibers ending on granule cells (in the contralateral hemisphere).
Memory tool

Climbing fibers climb from the Olive ("a vine climbing an olive tree") and grip one Purkinje cell like a vine on one trunk. Mossy fibers are the moss covering everything else.

Step 1 trap

Olivocerebellar (climbing) fibers enter through the inferior cerebellar peduncle, not the middle one. Only pontocerebellar fibers use the MCP.

CheckWhich spinal pathway carries upper-limb proprioception to the cerebellum, and what is its relay nucleus?
The cuneocerebellar tract, relayed in the accessory (lateral) cuneate nucleus of the medulla, entering via the ICP. (The dorsal spinocerebellar tract is its lower-limb counterpart, relayed in Clarke's nucleus.)
Step 1 HY
  • Cortex reaches the cerebellum only through relays: cortex → ipsilateral pontine nuclei → cross → contralateral MCP. There is no direct cortex–cerebellum connection.
  • Spinocerebellar tracts carry non-conscious proprioception (no thalamic relay, no awareness). Conscious proprioception travels in the dorsal columns (DCML).
  • Friedreich ataxia damages spinocerebellar tracts and dorsal columns, so ataxia comes with a positive Romberg.
Buzzwords

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1Damage to the right inferior olive would remove climbing-fiber input from which side of the cerebellum?
The left. Olivocerebellar fibers cross the midline in the medulla and enter the contralateral ICP. (Which is fine: the left cerebellum controls the left body and is "taught" by the right olive.)
A2A patient loses vibration and position sense in the legs and sways only when he closes his eyes. Is this a cerebellar input problem?
No: it is a dorsal column (conscious proprioception) problem, a positive Romberg. Vision compensates when the eyes are open. Cerebellar ataxia is present with eyes open.
A3Predict the main effect of losing all mossy-fiber input but keeping climbing fibers.
No ongoing context about limb position, head position or the cortical plan; the olive's error signal would have nothing to correct → severe ataxia. Mossy fibers carry nearly all the information.

Q3. Trace the cerebrocerebellar and spinocerebellar loops.

Cerebrocerebellum (lateral hemisphere → dentate)

  • In: motor/premotor cortex → pontine nuclei → contralateral MCP → lateral hemisphere. Extra input from the inferior olive (ICP).
  • Out: dentate → SCP → crosses in the midbrain →
    1. Reticular formation: tone and coordination
    2. Red nucleus: motor refinement
    3. VL thalamus → motor/premotor cortex (area 6)
    4. Inferior olive: feedback loop for motor learning
  • Role: planning, programming and timing of complex skilled movement (e.g., feeling around for an object you cannot see); motor learning.
  • Dorsal dentate → primary motor + premotor cortex (motor). Ventral dentate → prefrontal + posterior parietal cortex (non-motor/cognitive).

Spinocerebellum (vermis + paravermis)

  • In: spinocerebellar tracts (plus trigeminal; vermis also gets visual, auditory, vestibular input).
  • Vermis → fastigial → vestibular nuclei + reticular formation (vestibulospinal, reticulospinal = medial systems), also VA/VL → trunk motor cortex, and inferior olive → axial/proximal muscles, posture, balance.
  • Paravermis → interposed → SCP → contralateral red nucleus (rubrospinal = lateral system) and VA/VL → distal limb coordination.
  • Role: modulates ongoing movement using proprioceptive feedback.

Vestibulocerebellum (flocculonodular lobe ↔ vestibular nuclei, all via ICP)

  • Descending: modulates the vestibulospinal tract → axial/proximal muscles, posture, balance.
  • Ascending: modulates vestibulo-ocular pathways via the MLF to CN III, IV, VI → eye movements.
  • Lesion → nystagmus, truncal ataxia, postural instability.
  • Feedback control: compares planned vs executed movement → can only correct using recent past activity
    • Motor: overshoot when reaching
    • Cognitive: perseverating on a topic
  • Feedforward control: uses past experience to predict (associative learning)
    • Other people's motion: walking through a crowd
    • Speech: anticipating a punchline
CheckMatch each task to a division: (1) keeping your balance on a rocking boat, (2) a pianist planning a fast run, (3) adjusting your stride on uneven ground.
(1) Vestibulocerebellum; (2) cerebrocerebellum (dentate: planning/timing); (3) spinocerebellum (ongoing execution with spinal feedback).
Step 1 HY
  • Output pathway to cortex: deep nuclei → SCP → (decussation) → contralateral VL thalamus → motor cortex.
  • Medial = axial (fastigial, vestibulospinal/reticulospinal), lateral = limb (interposed → rubrospinal; dentate → thalamus → corticospinal).
  • Deep brain stimulation for disabling cerebellar/essential tremor targets the VL (Vim) thalamus, the relay for dentatothalamic fibers, not the cerebellar nuclei.
Step 1 trap

"The cerebellum projects to motor cortex" is true only via the thalamus. If an answer choice offers a direct cerebellum → cortex projection, or says the cerebellum projects directly to spinal motor neurons, it is wrong. Its influence on the cord is always through brainstem tracts or cortex.

Memory tool

Think of the cerebellum as a sports coach: the cerebrocerebellum draws up the play before the snap, the spinocerebellum shouts corrections during the play, and the vestibulocerebellum keeps the players on their feet. The inferior olive is the video review that teaches next time.

ApplyLO 4 application questions

A1A patient with a disabling cerebellar intention tremor improves after deep brain stimulation. Where was the electrode most likely placed?
Ventral lateral (VL / Vim) nucleus of the thalamus, the relay for cerebellar (dentatothalamic) output to motor cortex.
A2Why do cerebellar patients overshoot a target, then correct back and forth?
Feedback correction relies on recent past activity; with a damaged cerebellum the "brake" and prediction come too late, so the limb overshoots, then over-corrects (dysmetria, intention tremor).
A3A lesion of the SCP just before its decussation would interrupt output from which nuclei?
Dentate and interposed nuclei (to red nucleus and VL thalamus). Fastigial and flocculonodular outputs travel mainly in the ICP to the vestibular nuclei and reticular formation.
A4A patient with a posterior-lobe lesion names only 6 animals in 60 seconds and has trouble with planning. Which output channel explains this?
Ventral dentate → thalamus → prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex (non-motor domain). More than half of the cerebellum is wired to prefrontal/parietal cortex: the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome.

The inputs obey the same rule: spinal tracts describing the right body reach the right cerebellum (dorsal spinocerebellar/cuneocerebellar stay ipsilateral; ventral spinocerebellar crosses twice), and the left cortex talks to the right cerebellum through the crossed pontocerebellar fibers. So: right cerebellum = right body.

CheckA left cerebellar hemisphere stroke. Which arm shows the intention tremor, and which motor cortex is "receiving bad timing"?
Left arm; the right motor cortex (via the right VL thalamus) receives the faulty cerebellar signal and drives the left arm through the crossed corticospinal tract.
Step 1 HY — laterality
  • Cerebellar hemisphere lesion → ipsilateral limb ataxia; patient falls/veers toward the side of the lesion.
  • Cerebral cortex lesion → contralateral weakness. Cerebellar lesion → ipsilateral incoordination without true weakness.
  • Midline (vermis) lesion → bilateral, mostly truncal signs.
Step 1 trap

A lesion of the SCP after its decussation (or of the VL thalamus / red nucleus) gives contralateral ataxia relative to that lesion, because you are past the first crossing. Likewise, a lesion of the pons/MCP causes ataxia ipsilateral to the MCP, but the pontine nuclei themselves relay the contralateral cortex. Always ask: "which side of which crossing?"

Memory tool

"Two wrongs make a right": two crossings → the right cerebellum runs the right side. Or: the cerebellum is the "same-side syndrome."

ApplyLO 5 application questions

A1A lacunar infarct destroys the left VL thalamus. On which side would cerebellar-type incoordination appear, if any?
Right side. The left VL relays the right cerebellum's output to the left motor cortex, which controls the right body (only one crossing remains: the pyramidal decussation).
A2A patient veers to the left when walking and has dysmetria of the left arm and leg. Where is the lesion?
Left cerebellar hemisphere. Hemispheric lesions are ipsilateral, and patients drift toward the side of the lesion.
A3A midline vermis tumor: unilateral or bilateral signs?
Bilateral, mostly truncal (wide-based gait, sitting/standing unsteadiness); limbs relatively spared. The vermis controls both sides of the trunk.

Memory tool — DANISH

Dysdiadochokinesia, Ataxia, Nystagmus, Intention tremor, Scanning (slurred) speech, Hypotonia (and heel-to-shin / finger-to-nose dysmetria).

Step 1 HY — Romberg
  • Romberg sign tests conscious proprioception (dorsal columns): stable with eyes open, falls with eyes closed = positive.
  • Cerebellar ataxia is unsteady even with eyes open and is not dramatically worsened by eye closure, so Romberg is classically negative (or cannot be done). Vision cannot replace a cerebellum.
  • Positive Romberg + ataxia: B12 deficiency, tabes dorsalis, Friedreich ataxia, sensory neuropathy.
Step 1 trap

Dysdiadochokinesia + ataxia → think cerebellum, not basal ganglia. Basal ganglia disease gives resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia or involuntary movements (chorea, hemiballismus), not dysmetria. Cerebellar patients have normal strength, sensation and reflexes (reflexes may be pendular, never hyperreflexic).

Q2. What do anterior, posterior and flocculonodular lobe lesions look like?

Anterior lobe syndrome spinocerebellum

Lower-limb and gait ataxia ("walks as though drunk"), truncal ataxia from vermis involvement (unsteady even when the trunk is supported), only slight upper-limb ataxia. Classic cause: chronic alcohol (anterior-superior vermis).

Posterior lobe syndrome usually ipsilateral

Ataxia of both arm and leg on one side (pulled to that side "as if by a magnet" when walking). If the dentate is involved: dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor, scanning speech. Plus the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome.

Flocculonodular syndrome vestibulocerebellum

Disturbed balance: loss of equilibrium, tendency to fall over, nystagmus / eye-movement problems. Limb coordination is spared (no dysmetria lying down).

CheckWhat is the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome?
Non-motor deficits from posterior-lobe lesions: impaired executive function, verbal fluency, working memory, visuospatial skills and blunted/disinhibited affect. The cerebellum applies "how far, how fast, when to stop" to thought and language too.
Step 1 trap

Some summaries say "anterior lobe = upper limb." Go with the body map: the anterior lobe represents the trunk and proximal limbs (posture, gait), and anterior lobe syndrome is leg/gait + truncal ataxia with only slight arm involvement.

Buzzwords
"wide-based, drunken gait, arms spared" → anterior vermis (alcohol)"falls toward the side of the lesion" → ipsilateral hemisphere"can't sit or stand unsupported, nystagmus" → flocculonodular / vermis"staccato / scanning speech" → lateral hemisphere; MS"worse as finger approaches nose" → intention tremor (dentate)
Q3. The case: a "drunk driver" with zero blood alcohol. Where is the lesion?

Case

A 35-year-old woman is stopped on suspicion of drunk driving: slurred speech, imbalance even with eyes open, can't walk heel-to-toe or stand on one leg, nystagmus on lateral gaze, worst looking right, repeatedly misses her nose with the right hand. Blood alcohol 0, drug screen negative. The neurologist adds: onset over 2 weeks; right dysmetria and intention tremor; right dysdiadochokinesia; staccato speech; low verbal fluency (6 animals in 60 s); normal strength, sensation, reflexes.

FindingPoints to

Localization: a right posterior-lobe lesion (vermis/paravermis + lateral hemisphere with the dentate), plus a flocculonodular component.

Diagnosis: multiple sclerosis: demyelinating plaque in the right cerebellar hemisphere centered on the dentate, extending into the right MCP and right side of the brainstem.

CheckWhy are all of her limb signs on the right, if the plaque is in the right cerebellum?
The double crossing: right dentate → SCP decussation → left VL thalamus → left motor cortex → pyramidal decussation → right limbs.
Step 1 HY — MS

MS loves the cerebellum, its peduncles and the brainstem. Charcot triad: Scanning speech, Intention tremor, Nystagmus ("SIN"). Also look for optic neuritis, INO (MLF), periventricular plaques (Dawson fingers), oligoclonal bands in CSF.

Q4. Which cerebellar diseases does Step 1 love?

Alcoholic cerebellar degeneration

Anterior-superior vermis atrophy → wide-based gait and truncal ataxia, legs > arms. Acute intoxication: GABA-A potentiation → all cerebellar signs. Look for Wernicke (thiamine: ataxia + ophthalmoplegia + confusion).

Friedreich ataxia

AR GAA repeat, frataxin (chr 9), mitochondrial iron. Spinocerebellar tracts + dorsal columns + lateral CST + DRG: ataxia with positive Romberg, ↓ reflexes but Babinski +, pes cavus, kyphoscoliosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (cause of death), diabetes. Teens.

Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCA)

AD CAG (polyglutamine) repeats with anticipation; progressive adult-onset gait ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus; Purkinje cell loss.

Ataxia-telangiectasia

AR ATM (DNA double-strand break repair). Childhood cerebellar ataxia (Purkinje loss), oculocutaneous telangiectasias, IgA deficiency → sinopulmonary infections, ↑ AFP, ↑ lymphoma/leukemia, radiation sensitivity.

Medulloblastoma

Most common malignant pediatric brain tumor; vermis / 4th ventricle roof. Truncal ataxia, nystagmus, obstructive hydrocephalus (morning headache, vomiting, papilledema), drop metastases. Small round blue cells, Homer-Wright rosettes, synaptophysin +.

Other posterior fossa tumors

Pilocytic astrocytoma: child, cystic + mural nodule in a hemisphere, Rosenthal fibers, GFAP + (ipsilateral limb ataxia). Ependymoma: 4th ventricle, perivascular pseudorosettes. Hemangioblastoma: adult, VHL, EPO → polycythemia.

Cerebellar strokes

PICA: lateral medullary (Wallenberg) + inferior cerebellum → vertigo, nystagmus, ipsilateral limb ataxia, dysphagia/hoarseness, ipsilateral face + contralateral body pain/temp loss, Horner. AICA: lateral pons → same + facial paralysis, deafness. SCA artery: superior cerebellum → limb/gait ataxia. Hypertensive hemorrhage (Charcot-Bouchard) can compress the 4th ventricle: surgical emergency.

Chiari & Dandy-Walker

Chiari I: tonsils herniate >3-5 mm through foramen magnum; occipital headache, cerebellar signs, syringomyelia (cape-like pain/temp loss). Chiari II: vermis + tonsils, with lumbosacral myelomeningocele, hydrocephalus. Dandy-Walker: vermis agenesis, cystic 4th ventricle, enlarged posterior fossa.

Toxic / paraneoplastic

Phenytoin, carbamazepine, lithium toxicity → nystagmus, ataxia. Paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration: anti-Yo (ovarian/breast cancer, anti-Purkinje), anti-Hu/anti-P/Q (small cell lung). Hypothyroidism and B12 deficiency also cause ataxia.

Step 1 HY — tonsillar herniation

Raised posterior-fossa pressure pushes the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum → compress the medulla → cardiorespiratory arrest. Same structure that sags in Chiari I.

Step 1 trap

Child + posterior fossa mass: midline vermis, solid, drop mets → medulloblastoma; lateral hemisphere, cystic with mural nodule, good prognosis → pilocytic astrocytoma. Truncal vs limb signs tell you which.

Memory tool

Friedreich is "Fried" everywhere: fried tracts (spinocerebellar, dorsal columns, CST), fried heart (HCM), fried pancreas (diabetes), and a fried-out foot (pes cavus). GAA = "GAAit" problems.

ApplyLO 6 application questions

A1A 55-year-old man with long-standing alcohol use has a broad-based, unsteady gait and heel-to-shin ataxia, but finger-to-nose is nearly normal. Localize.
Anterior lobe / anterior-superior vermis (anterior lobe syndrome): legs and trunk > arms.
A2A 6-year-old has morning headaches, vomiting, and can't sit without swaying; nystagmus is present; finger-to-nose in bed is normal. Localize and name the likely tumor.
Flocculonodular lobe / posterior vermis; medulloblastoma, often obstructing the 4th ventricle → hydrocephalus.
A3Left dysdiadochokinesia and dysmetria, gait drifting left, no nystagmus, normal equilibrium when standing still. Localize.
Left lateral cerebellar hemisphere (dentate), with some spinocerebellar involvement for the gait. No nystagmus/normal equilibrium argues against flocculonodular involvement.
A4A 47-year-old with poorly controlled hypertension suddenly develops vertigo, vomiting, inability to walk and right-sided intention tremor, then becomes drowsy. Diagnosis and danger?
Right cerebellar hypertensive hemorrhage; risk of 4th ventricle compression → hydrocephalus and brainstem compression / tonsillar herniation → urgent neurosurgical decompression.
A5A teenager has progressive ataxia, absent ankle reflexes, upgoing toes, loss of vibration sense and an abnormal ECG. Why is his Romberg positive when most cerebellar patients' is not?
Friedreich ataxia also destroys the dorsal columns (conscious proprioception), so he gets worse with eyes closed; pure cerebellar ataxia is unsteady with eyes open too.

1A 68-year-old man has sudden vertigo and clumsiness. Exam shows past-pointing on finger-to-nose and an action tremor that worsens near the target, both in the right arm. Strength, sensation and reflexes are normal. MRI shows a small infarct. Where is it?

C. Dysmetria + intention tremor = lateral hemisphere (dentate), and cerebellar signs are ipsilateral (double crossing). A gives left-sided signs. B would cause contralateral (left) weakness, not incoordination. D: basal ganglia, resting tremor/bradykinesia. E: vermis lesions give bilateral truncal ataxia, not unilateral limb signs.

2A researcher labels axons that enter the cerebellum via the inferior cerebellar peduncle, ascend through the granular and Purkinje layers, and wrap around the dendritic tree of a single Purkinje cell. Where are the cell bodies of these axons?

E. These are climbing fibers (1:1 with a Purkinje cell, aspartate), which arise only from the inferior olive. A, B and C give rise to mossy fibers, which end on granule cells. D: granule cells give parallel fibers that contact many Purkinje cells, not one.

3A 58-year-old man with 30 years of heavy drinking has a slowly progressive, wide-based, staggering gait and cannot walk heel-to-toe. Heel-to-shin testing is abnormal bilaterally; finger-to-nose is nearly normal. Eye movements and speech are normal. Which region is most affected?

B. Chronic alcohol causes anterior vermis degeneration: bilateral gait and leg ataxia with arms relatively spared (the anterior lobe maps trunk and proximal limbs). A: nystagmus and imbalance. C: unilateral limb dysmetria/tremor. D: sensory ataxia with positive Romberg and loss of vibration/position sense. E: unilateral ipsilateral limb ataxia.

4A 5-year-old boy has 3 weeks of morning headaches and vomiting. He sways when sitting unsupported and has horizontal nystagmus; limb coordination is normal when tested lying down. MRI shows a solid midline mass arising from the roof of the 4th ventricle with hydrocephalus. Biopsy would most likely show:

D. Medulloblastoma: midline vermis/flocculonodular region in a child → truncal ataxia, nystagmus, obstructive hydrocephalus. A: pilocytic astrocytoma (usually cystic, in a hemisphere). B: meningioma. C: ependymoma (arises from the 4th ventricle floor/ependyma; possible, but a solid vermian mass in a 5-year-old with this picture is classic medulloblastoma). E: oligodendroglioma (adult, frontal lobe).

5A 13-year-old girl has 2 years of progressive clumsiness and falls. Exam: dysarthria, absent ankle jerks, bilateral extensor plantar responses, loss of vibration sense in the feet, positive Romberg, pes cavus and kyphoscoliosis. ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy. The underlying defect is:

A. Friedreich ataxia (AR): spinocerebellar tracts + dorsal columns (positive Romberg) + corticospinal (Babinski) + DRG (areflexia), with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. B: Huntington disease (chorea, AD). C: ataxia-telangiectasia (telangiectasias, IgA deficiency, no cardiomyopathy). D: Duchenne (proximal weakness, not ataxia). E: paraneoplastic anti-Yo (adults with ovarian/breast cancer).

6A 50-year-old woman is unsteady with a wide-based gait. She is just as unsteady with eyes open as with eyes closed. Vibration and joint-position sense are normal in all limbs. Which statement best explains her findings?

D. Cerebellar (truncal) ataxia is present with eyes open; vision cannot compensate, so a true positive Romberg (stable open, falls closed) is absent. A and B are sensory ataxias: positive Romberg with abnormal vibration/position sense. C: vestibular loss is compensated by vision, so she would be much worse with eyes closed. E: spastic weakness, hyperreflexia, not ataxia.

7A demyelinating plaque is confined to the left middle cerebellar peduncle. Which fibers are interrupted?

B. The MCP carries only pontocerebellar input, from the contralateral pontine nuclei. A travels in the SCP. C and D enter via the ICP. E leaves via the ICP (juxtarestiform portion).

8Purkinje cells of the lateral cerebellar hemisphere send their axons to which structure?

E. Lateral hemisphere → dentate (cerebrocerebellum). A receives the vermis; B (with emboliform) receives the paravermis. C and D are targets of the deep nuclei; Purkinje cells never leave the cerebellum (except flocculonodular → vestibular nuclei).

9Which statement about the only neuron that carries signals out of the cerebellar cortex is correct?

A. The Purkinje cell is GABAergic and inhibits the deep nuclei (and vestibular nuclei). B describes deep-nucleus output. C: mossy fibers reach Purkinje cells only indirectly via granule cells/parallel fibers. D: Purkinje cell bodies form their own single layer. E describes the granule cell.

10A 64-year-old woman develops sudden vertigo, nausea, hoarseness and difficulty swallowing. Exam: left Horner syndrome, loss of pain and temperature on the left face and right body, nystagmus, and left arm and leg ataxia with normal strength. Occlusion of which artery?

C. Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome from left PICA (or vertebral) occlusion: nucleus ambiguus (hoarseness, dysphagia), crossed pain/temp loss, Horner, vestibular nuclei (vertigo, nystagmus) and ICP/inferior cerebellum → ipsilateral ataxia. A: lateral pons, would add facial paralysis and hearing loss. B: wrong side. D: superior cerebellum, limb ataxia without medullary signs. E: locked-in syndrome or coma.

11A 29-year-old woman with a prior episode of painful vision loss in one eye now has scanning speech, nystagmus and a coarse tremor of the right hand that worsens as she reaches for a cup. Interruption of which pathway best explains the tremor?

D. MS with Charcot triad (scanning speech, intention tremor, nystagmus). Intention tremor = dentate/SCP (cerebellar outflow), ipsilateral to the right-hand tremor. A: resting tremor (Parkinson). B: sensory ataxia with positive Romberg. C: weakness, spasticity. E: INO (eye adduction deficit), not limb tremor.

12A child cannot stand or walk without falling and has nystagmus, but finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin are normal while lying in bed. Purkinje cells of the damaged region project directly to which structure?

E. This is the flocculonodular (vestibulocerebellar) syndrome; its Purkinje cells project directly to the vestibular nuclei, which serve as its deep nucleus. A: lateral hemisphere. B: paravermis. C and D are targets of deep nuclei, not of Purkinje cells.

13After resection of a posterior-lobe cerebellar tumor, a 40-year-old teacher has mild limb ataxia but also struggles to plan lessons, names only a few words starting with "F" in a minute, and seems emotionally flat. Which best explains her non-motor deficits?

B. Cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome: posterior lobe lesions disrupt the non-motor dentate domain (ventral dentate → thalamus → prefrontal/posterior parietal cortex), causing executive, verbal fluency and affective deficits. A: balance and nystagmus. C: proprioceptive input for limbs. D would give global signs of raised ICP, not this selective pattern. E: vermis = axial motor control.

14A 72-year-old man has a disabling tremor of the left arm that appears when he reaches for objects and worsens near the target, due to a remote left cerebellar stroke. Medication fails. Deep brain stimulation is planned. The most appropriate target is the:

A. Cerebellar outflow from the left dentate crosses in the SCP decussation to the right VL thalamus, which projects to the right motor cortex controlling the left arm. B is the wrong side. C: the cerebellar nuclei are not the DBS target. D: STN is a Parkinson disease target. E: not a DBS target.
