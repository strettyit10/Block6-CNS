Memory tool — 10 / 0.5 / 0.2

Q2. Opiate vs opioid: is there a difference?

Opiate

A compound derived from opium (morphine, codeine, papaverine). Describes origin.

Opioid

Any compound active at opioid receptors: natural, semisynthetic, synthetic, endogenous peptides, whose opioid-like effects are blocked by naloxone. Describes action.

CheckIs fentanyl an opiate, an opioid, or both?
Opioid only. It is fully synthetic, so it is not derived from opium, but it acts at μ receptors and naloxone blocks it.
Q3. What are the body's own opioids, and which receptor does each prefer?
  • Three peptide families, each cut from its own precursor gene:
    • β-endorphin ← pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC; the same precursor that gives ACTH and MSH)
    • Enkephalins (met-, leu-) ← preproenkephalin
    • Dynorphins ← preprodynorphin
  • All share the N-terminal "opioid motif" Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-(Met/Leu).
  • Three classic receptors: μ (μ1,2), κ (κ1,2,3), δ (δ1,2), plus the opioid-like ORL1 (ligand = orphanin / nociceptin). Opioid drugs act through μ, κ and δ, but most pharmacologic effects of opioid drugs are mediated by μ.

Kappa, κ1,2,3 (KOR)Dynorphin AMostly spinal analgesia; dysphoria / psychotomimetic effects; diuresis (↓ ADH); less respiratory depression and miosis

CheckA new drug gives spinal analgesia and strong diuresis but makes volunteers dysphoric and hallucinating. Which receptor does it target?
κ (kappa). Spinal analgesia + diuresis + psychotomimetic/dysphoric effects is the κ signature (dynorphin's receptor).
Step 1 HY — receptor ↔ ligand pairs

Boards pair them one-to-one: μ = β-endorphin, δ = enkephalin, κ = dynorphin. All opioid receptors are Gi/o-coupled → ↓ cAMP. β-endorphin comes from POMC, the same precursor as ACTH and MSH.

Memory tool

"Mu = MOrphine; Kappa = Kidneys + Krazy" (κ → diuresis + psychotomimesis). Dynorphin → "Dysphoria"; Delta → "Deep in the spine" (peptides need intraspinal delivery).

The dose-response picture for this lives in LO 6 (ceiling effect and precipitated withdrawal).

CheckWhy can a partial agonist reduce the effect in someone already saturated with a full agonist?
It has high affinity but low efficacy: it kicks the full agonist off the receptor and replaces a large signal with a smaller one, so net μ signaling falls. In a dependent patient this feels like withdrawal.
Step 1 trap

"Partial agonist" is not "weak drug". Buprenorphine binds μ more tightly than morphine; its efficacy is what is limited. High affinity + low efficacy is exactly why it both blocks heroin and precipitates withdrawal.

ApplyLO 1 application questions

A1A chemist wants a starting material to make buprenorphine and the opioid antagonists. Which opium alkaloid does she pick?
Thebaine. It has almost no clinical use itself, but it is the semisynthetic precursor for buprenorphine, oxycodone and the naloxone/naltrexone family (via oxymorphone).
A2A volunteer is given a selective δ-peptide agonist intravenously and feels no analgesia. Why, and how could you deliver it?
Peptide δ agonists do not cross the BBB. They would need intraspinal (intrathecal) delivery to reach their target.
A3Naloxone fails to reverse an effect produced by a novel analgesic. Which receptor is the most likely target?
ORL1 (nociceptin/orphanin FQ receptor). It is opioid-like but naloxone-insensitive. (Some psychotomimetic effects of pentazocine are also not naloxone-reversible.)

LO 2Receptor signaling and how opioids block painGi/o → ↓ cAMP, ↓ Ca²⁺, ↑ K⁺, at spinal and supraspinal sites

By the end: you can draw the μ-receptor cascade at a dorsal horn synapse (pre- and postsynaptic), explain why C-fiber (dull) pain responds better than Aδ (sharp) pain, and name the two brain actions that add to spinal analgesia.
Q1. What happens inside a neuron when an opioid binds the μ receptor?

The μ receptor is a Gi/o-coupled GPCR. Binding splits the G protein into Gαi/o and Gβγ, and each subunit has a job:

Opioid binds μGi/o activated
→
Gαi/oinhibits adenylyl cyclase → ↓ cAMP / PKA
→
Gβγ (pre)closes voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → ↓ transmitter release
→
Gβγ (post)opens K⁺ (GIRK) channels → K⁺ efflux → hyperpolarization
  • μ receptor (GPCR) → inhibits adenylyl cyclase → ↓ cAMP → ↓ presynaptic Ca²⁺ channel opening → ↓ transmitter release; and stimulates postsynaptic K⁺ efflux → hyperpolarization.
  • Analgesia = the G-protein arm (↓ cAMP/PKA and ↓ synaptic transmission + postsynaptic K⁺ channel stimulation).
  • The μ receptor also drives β-arrestin signaling → tolerance + respiratory depression + constipation (know all three).
CheckTrue or false: opioids inhibit postsynaptic K⁺ channels, depolarizing the 2nd-order neuron.
False. Opioids open (activate) postsynaptic K⁺ channels → K⁺ leaves → hyperpolarization. The channel they close is the presynaptic Ca²⁺ channel.
Step 1 trap — which channel goes which way?

Pre = Ca²⁺ closed. Post = K⁺ opened. Distractors flip these, or offer "Na⁺ channel block" (that is local anesthetics) or "Cl⁻ channel opening" (that is GABAA/benzodiazepines).

Memory tool

"Opioids make neurons Calm and Klosed": Ca²⁺ in stops, K⁺ out goes. Both moves push the neuron away from firing.

Q2. Where in the spinal cord do opioids act, and why is dull pain relieved better than sharp pain?
  • Site = dorsal horn (substantia gelatinosa), the first synapse of the pain pathway:
    • μ receptors on central terminals of primary afferents → presynaptic inhibition
    • μ receptors on 2nd-order (spinothalamic) neurons → postsynaptic inhibition
    • Local enkephalin interneurons are switched on by descending fibers from the brainstem and do the same thing naturally
  • Opioids preferentially inhibit C-fiber nociceptive transmission ("C more than Aδ" → better for chronic, dull pain):
    • C fibers = unmyelinated, slow → continuous, dull, aching, burning (chronic) pain → well relieved
    • Aδ fibers = thinly myelinated, fast → sharp, intermittent, well-localized pain → less sensitive
    • Continuous dull pain is relieved more effectively than sharp intermittent pain
    • At high enough doses opioids still relieve severe pain such as renal or biliary colic
  • Giving morphine at spinal and supraspinal sites together is synergistic (~10-fold lower total dose for the same analgesia).
FiberMyelin / speedPain qualityOpioid sensitivity
CUnmyelinated, slow (0.5–2 m/s)Dull, aching, burning, persistentHigh
AδThin myelin, fast (5–30 m/s)Sharp, pricking, first painLower
CheckWhich pain responds better to morphine: the constant ache of a bone metastasis or the brief jab of a needle stick?
The constant ache. It is carried mainly by C fibers, which opioids inhibit preferentially.
Buzzwords
"substantia gelatinosa" → dorsal horn opioid site "substance P + glutamate" → C-fiber transmitters opioids reduce "GIRK" → K⁺ channel opened by Gβγ "dull, burning, constant" → C fiber → opioid-sensitive
Q3. What do opioids do in the brain to add to spinal analgesia?
  1. Excite descending inhibitory pathways: opioids activate the periaqueductal gray (PAG) → rostral ventromedial medulla (raphe) → fibers down to the dorsal horn that suppress incoming pain signals. (Opioids do this by disinhibiting PAG output neurons, i.e., silencing local GABA neurons.)
  2. Reduce the emotional component: in limbic areas (the medial pain system, i.e., the medial anterolateral system) they reduce the "suffering", producing indifference to pain. Patients often say "the pain is still there, but it doesn't bother me".
  • Opioids act in both brain and spinal cord: PAG activation + reduced emotional pain transmission → indifference to pain.
  • Opioids produce more analgesia when combined with NSAIDs (central + peripheral action).
CheckName the two analgesic actions of opioids in the brain.
(1) Excite the PAG descending inhibitory pathway to the spinal cord; (2) reduce the emotional "suffering" component → indifference to pain.
Step 1 HY — why combine opioids with NSAIDs or acetaminophen

NSAIDs act peripherally (↓ prostaglandins → less nociceptor sensitization); opioids act centrally (dorsal horn + brain). Different steps → additive/synergistic analgesia at lower doses of each: codeine + acetaminophen (Tylenol #3), hydrocodone + acetaminophen (Vicodin), oxycodone + acetaminophen (Percocet) or + aspirin (Percodan). Watch the acetaminophen ceiling (hepatotoxicity) in combination pills.

ApplyLO 2 application questions

A1A researcher records from a dorsal horn neuron. After morphine, the same C-fiber stimulus releases less glutamate and the neuron's resting potential drops from −65 to −72 mV. Explain both findings.
Less glutamate = presynaptic Gβγ closing voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels on the C-fiber terminal. More negative potential = postsynaptic Gβγ opening GIRK K⁺ channels → K⁺ efflux → hyperpolarization.
A2A patient with a burn on the forearm says morphine "took away the deep throbbing but I still feel the sharp sting when the dressing is touched." Why?
Opioids preferentially inhibit C fibers (deep, throbbing, persistent pain) over Aδ fibers (sharp, well-localized pain).
A3After a post-op dose of morphine a patient says, "I can still feel it, I just don't care anymore." Which action explains this?
Supraspinal action on limbic / medial pain pathways → reduced emotional "suffering" = indifference to pain.
A4A drug selectively blocks β-arrestin recruitment to the μ receptor but leaves G-protein signaling intact. Predict its profile.
Analgesia preserved (G-protein arm), with less tolerance, respiratory depression and constipation (β-arrestin arm). This is the rationale for "biased" μ agonists now in development.

Q2. How do opioids cause euphoria and sedation?
  • Euphoria: μ receptors sit on GABA interneurons in the VTA. Silencing them removes the brake on dopamine neurons → ↑ DA in the nucleus accumbens (mesolimbic reward pathway) → reinforcing quality of opioids. (Linked to β-arrestin signaling.)
  • Sedation and anxiolysis: μ inhibition of norepinephrine release from the locus coeruleus → reduced anxiety, drowsiness, lethargy, "mental clouding", poor concentration, tranquility. (Morphine ← Morpheus, Greek god of dreams.)
CheckWhy does opioid withdrawal cause anxiety, tachycardia and sweating?
Chronic opioids suppress locus coeruleus NE firing; neurons up-regulate cAMP to compensate. Remove the opioid → locus coeruleus NE surge → sympathetic overdrive. That is why clonidine/lofexidine (α2 agonists that shut the LC down) help withdrawal.
Memory tool

"Two negatives make a high": opioid ⊣ GABA ⊣ dopamine = more dopamine. "LC = Less Calm" when opioids leave: the locus coeruleus fires and you get withdrawal jitters.

Q3. Why is respiratory depression the effect that kills?
  • Death from opioid poisoning is nearly always respiratory arrest.
  • Mechanism (brainstem):
    • ↓ responsiveness of brainstem respiratory centers to CO₂ (the main drive)
    • Depression of pontine and medullary rhythm centers
  • What you see: ↓ rate (even at therapeutic doses; may fall to 3–4 breaths/min), ↓ tidal and minute volume, irregular/periodic breathing.
  • Linked to β-arrestin signaling.
  • Timing of peak depression after morphine: 5–10 min IV, ~30 min IM, ~90 min SC.
  • Higher risk with:
    • Other CNS depressants: benzodiazepines (FDA black-box warning for the combination), alcohol, sedative-hypnotics, general anesthetics
    • ↓ respiratory reserve: COPD, asthma, frail elderly, head injury
  • Clinically significant depression is uncommon at standard doses in people with normal lungs, and it is fully reversed by naloxone.

Step 1 HY — the opioid overdose triad

Coma (CNS depression) + pinpoint pupils (miosis) + respiratory depression = opioid toxicity until proven otherwise → give naloxone. Other clues: track marks, bradycardia, hypothermia, ↓ bowel sounds, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema. A patient with this triad who also seizes may have taken tramadol or meperidine.

Step 1 trap — pinpoint pupils without opioids

Miosis also occurs with organophosphate/cholinergic toxicity (but with SLUDGE, bronchorrhea, fasciculations) and pontine hemorrhage. Large pupils argue against pure opioid overdose: think anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, or meperidine (may not cause miosis). Hypoxic brain injury after an overdose can also leave pupils dilated.

Q4. What causes nausea, constipation and the GI effects?

Nausea & vomiting

  • Direct stimulation of the chemoreceptor trigger zone in the area postrema (medulla, outside the BBB)
  • ↑ vestibular sensitivity → worse when moving
  • Nausea ~40%, vomiting ~15% of ambulatory patients; uncommon when lying down
  • Tolerance develops; can treat like motion sickness

Constipation

  • ↑ intestinal tone and spasm (non-propulsive contractions)
  • ↓ motility and propulsion → slow transit
  • More water absorbed → dehydrated feces
  • ↓ gastric emptying, ↓ GI secretions, ↑ anal sphincter tone
  • β-arrestin linked; NO tolerance → major side effect of chronic therapy
  • Because they are so constipating, opioids are the most effective antidiarrheals:
    • Loperamide (Imodium): μ agonist with limited CNS entry (P-glycoprotein pumps it out of the brain) → OTC
    • Diphenoxylate (Lomotil): GI-selective opioid, formulated with atropine to deter abuse (high doses → unpleasant anticholinergic effects)
  • Biliary tract: sphincter of Oddi spasm → ↑ common bile duct pressure → biliary colic-like pain.
  • Urinary: ↑ sphincter tone, inhibits voiding reflex → urinary retention (sometimes needs catheter).
CheckWhy is atropine added to diphenoxylate?
Abuse deterrence. At normal doses the atropine does little; if someone takes a large dose to get high, the anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, tachycardia, blurred vision) make it unpleasant.
Buzzwords
"area postrema / CTZ" → opioid nausea "worse when walking, gone lying down" → vestibular component "Lomotil + atropine" → diphenoxylate, abuse deterrent "sphincter of Oddi spasm" → morphine biliary pain "3–4 breaths/min" → opioid respiratory depression
Q5. Why pinpoint pupils, itching, low blood pressure and hormonal changes?
  • Miosis: morphine and most μ and κ agonists excite the parasympathetic nerve to the pupil (Edinger-Westphal nucleus → CN III → sphincter pupillae). Some tolerance develops, but people with high circulating opioid levels keep pinpoint pupils.
  • Pruritus and flushing: histamine release from mast cells (morphine > fentanyl) + changes in C-fiber activity (scratching = noxious stimulus that relieves itch; opioids alter this balance).
  • Cardiovascular: peripheral vasodilation, ↓ resistance, blunted baroreceptor reflex → orthostatic hypotension when the patient sits up (histamine plays a big role; antihistamines may not reverse it, naloxone may be needed). Morphine also venodilates → ↓ preload (historic use in acute pulmonary edema).
  • Neuroendocrine: ↓ GnRH and CRF → ↓ LH, FSH, ACTH → ↓ testosterone and cortisol → altered menstruation, libido, fertility; μ agonists ↑ prolactin. κ agonists ↓ ADH (diuresis); μ agonists tend to be antidiuretic.
  • Convulsions: rare in humans at high doses (morphine inhibits GABA release onto hippocampal pyramidal cells → disinhibition). More relevant for meperidine (normeperidine), tramadol and morphine's M3G.
  • Immune: immunosuppressive effects (mostly δ receptor, plus central sympathetic/HPA effects).
CheckA patient flushes and itches after IV morphine. Is this an IgE allergy?
Usually not. Morphine causes direct, non-IgE mast-cell histamine release (pseudoallergy). Switching to a less histamine-releasing opioid such as fentanyl usually helps.
Step 1 HY — when to be careful
  • Head injury → ↑ ICP via CO₂ retention; masks neuro exam
  • COPD/asthma → respiratory depression; histamine bronchospasm
  • Hypotension/hypovolemia → histamine vasodilation worsens it
  • Biliary colic/pancreatitis → sphincter of Oddi spasm (classic teaching)
  • BPH → urinary retention
  • Benzodiazepines/alcohol → additive respiratory depression (black box)
Memory tool — "MORPHINE" side effects

Miosis · Out of it (sedation) · Respiratory depression · Pruritus · Hypotension (histamine) · Increased biliary/urinary sphincter tone · Nausea · Emesis... and constipation that never goes away.

ApplyLO 3 application questions

A1A 24-year-old is found unresponsive with RR 4/min and 1-mm pupils. Diagnosis and first drug?
Opioid overdose (triad: coma, miosis, respiratory depression) → naloxone (IN, IM or IV) while supporting ventilation.
A2A post-op patient on a morphine PCA feels nauseated every time she walks to the bathroom but is fine in bed. Why?
Opioids stimulate the CTZ and increase vestibular sensitivity; nausea is common in ambulatory patients and uncommon when recumbent.
A3A man on chronic high-dose opioids has low libido, erectile dysfunction and low morning testosterone. Mechanism?
Opioids act on the hypothalamus to ↓ GnRH → ↓ LH/FSH → ↓ testosterone (opioid-induced hypogonadism).
A4A patient takes alprazolam for anxiety and is prescribed oxycodone after surgery. What is the main danger?
Additive CNS and respiratory depression, the subject of an FDA black-box warning for opioids + benzodiazepines. Reconsider doses or taper the benzodiazepine.

LO 4Tolerance, dependence, withdrawal and addictionWhat fades, what doesn't, and what happens when the drug stops

By the end: you can define all four terms, name the two effects with no tolerance, predict what changes when a dose is escalated, recognize withdrawal (spontaneous vs precipitated), and explain why kinetics drive addiction and treatment.
Q1. What is tolerance, and to which effects does it NOT develop?
  • Tolerance = functional desensitization: with continued use opioids become less potent, so a higher dose is needed for the same response.
  • All effects of opioids display tolerance EXCEPT constipation and miosis. When morphine is increased gradually: constipation worsens (no tolerance), initial nausea goes away, physical dependence occurs, and respiratory depression does NOT occur.
  • It can be fast: a clinical morphine dose of ~30 mg may rise to ~300 mg in as little as 10 days.
  • Mechanism unclear; β-arrestin signaling (receptor desensitization and internalization) is implicated. NMDA (glutamate) signaling also contributes: NMDA antagonists block morphine tolerance in animals (one reason methadone's NMDA block is useful).

Tolerance develops (fades with time)

  • Analgesia
  • Euphoria
  • Sedation, mental clouding
  • Nausea / vomiting
  • Respiratory depression
  • Cough suppression

NO tolerance (persists, worsens with dose)

  • Constipation
  • Miosis

Even small doses cause them, and they only get worse as the dose rises. Every chronic opioid prescription needs a bowel regimen.

CheckA cancer patient's morphine is raised 10-fold over several weeks. Which is LEAST likely: worse constipation, physical dependence, fading nausea, or new respiratory depression?
New respiratory depression is least likely: she has become tolerant to it as the dose rose gradually. Constipation worsens (no tolerance), she becomes physically dependent, and her early nausea fades.
Memory tool

"You never get used to the Pupils or the Poop." (Pinpoint pupils + constipation persist.)

Step 1 trap

A long-term user who still has pinpoint pupils does not prove a fresh overdose: miosis persists with chronic high levels. Conversely, tolerance to respiratory depression is lost quickly after abstinence (jail, rehab) → relapse at the old dose is a classic fatal overdose.

CheckWhich finding best shows a patient is physically dependent on morphine (not necessarily addicted)?
Withdrawal symptoms when the opioid is stopped abruptly. Needing more drug = tolerance; using despite harm = addiction.
Q3. What does opioid withdrawal look like, and how long does it last?

Withdrawal is the mirror image of the drug: whatever the opioid suppressed comes roaring back.

Opioid effect→Withdrawal sign
Euphoria→Dysphoria, craving, irritability
Tranquility, sedation→Anxiety, restlessness, insomnia, yawning
Constipation→Diarrhea, abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting
Miosis→Mydriasis
Analgesia→Pain, myalgias, arthralgias
↓ secretions, ↓ sympathetic→Sweating, rhinorrhea, lacrimation, piloerection ("cold turkey"), tachycardia, mild HTN
  • Classic presentation = abdominal pain, sweating, diarrhea.
  • Triggered by abruptly stopping the opioid or giving an opioid antagonist (opioid receptor blockers can cause withdrawal symptoms).
  • Miserable but rarely life-threatening in adults (unlike alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, which can cause seizures and death). Neonates of dependent mothers get neonatal abstinence syndrome.
  • Treatment: replace with a long-acting opioid and taper (methadone or buprenorphine); clonidine / lofexidine (α2 agonists) for autonomic symptoms.
CheckWhich set fits opioid withdrawal: (A) abdominal pain, respiratory depression, diarrhea; (B) pinpoint pupils, sweating, diarrhea; (C) abdominal pain, sweating, constipation; (D) abdominal pain, sweating, diarrhea?
D. Respiratory depression, pinpoint pupils and constipation are signs of opioid effect, not withdrawal.
Step 1 HY — withdrawal vs intoxication by the pupils

Intoxication = small pupils, slow, sleepy, constipated. Withdrawal = big pupils, yawning, runny nose, tears, goosebumps, diarrhea. Opioid withdrawal is not fatal in adults; the danger is relapse after lost tolerance. Rx: methadone/buprenorphine; clonidine or lofexidine for sympathetic symptoms.

Q2. Morphine: what limits it, how is it metabolized, and why avoid it in kidney failure?
  • Structure: OH groups at C3 and C6 make it hydrophilic → slower CNS penetration than lipophilic opioids. The N-CH₃ is important for activity.
  • High first-pass metabolism → oral bioavailability only ~25% (oral dose ≈ 3× the injected dose).
  • Metabolism: conjugated with glucuronic acid (liver, kidney, brain):
    • Morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G) ~60%: inactive at opioid receptors but neuroexcitatory (CNS stimulant → myoclonus, seizures)
    • Morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G) ~10%: active, ~2× more potent than morphine
    • Minor: N-demethylation → normorphine (inactive)
  • Elimination: morphine, M6G and M3G are all excreted by the kidneys → in renal impairment they accumulate → prolonged sedation, respiratory depression (M6G) and seizures (M3G) (chronic use → build-up of active metabolite).
    • Do NOT give morphine in renal insufficiency; choose an opioid without renally cleared active metabolites: fentanyl (no active metabolites) or hydromorphone / oxycodone (oral or IV). Methadone is an option but not first line.
    • Hepatic dysfunction → start opioids at ~50% of the usual dose.
  • Routes: oral, IM, IV, SC, epidural, intrathecal. Oral onset is slower.
  • Duration: 4–5 h (oral, IM, IV, SC); up to 24 h epidural/intrathecal, but being hydrophilic it lingers in CSF and spreads rostrally → delayed respiratory depression.
  • Limitations summary: low oral bioavailability, poor CNS penetration, high abuse potential.
CheckTRUE about morphine: (a) rapidly penetrates the CNS, (b) must be demethylated to be active, (c) its diacetylated form is inactive, (d) its oral bioavailability is limited by first-pass metabolism?
(d). Morphine is hydrophilic (slow CNS entry), active as is (codeine is the one needing demethylation), and diacetylmorphine (heroin) is very active.
Step 1 trap

"M3G is the active one" because 3 comes first? No. M6G = active (6 is "sicks" you with sedation); M3G = inactive at μ but excitatory. Also, morphine is not CYP2D6-dependent and is unaffected by SSRIs; codeine is.

Q3. Codeine: why is it weak, and why does CYP2D6 matter so much?
  • Codeine = 3-methylmorphine: the CH₃O at C3 kills receptor activity → a prodrug with very low μ affinity.
  • Analgesia comes from O-demethylation to morphine by CYP2D6 (~10% of a dose).
  • Weak because of low potency at μ receptors: only 10–15% as potent as morphine (~1/12); works slower than morphine; clinically about as strong as aspirin or acetaminophen alone. Better oral bioavailability than morphine but slower onset.
  • Usually combined with acetaminophen or aspirin (Tylenol #3). Strong antitussive (may involve sites that bind codeine itself).
  • Pharmacogenetics:
    • ~10% of Caucasians are poor metabolizers → little or no analgesia
    • ~1–2% carry multiple gene copies (ultrarapid metabolizers) → too much morphine → toxicity
  • Drug interactions: CYP2D6 inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine, bupropion, quinidine) block codeine analgesia. Morphine and oxycodone are unaffected by an SSRI, but the codeine effect may decrease.

Q4. Heroin and the semisynthetic analogs: what makes them more potent?

Heroin (diacetylmorphine)

Acetyl groups at C3 and C6 → much more lipid-soluble → crosses BBB fast. A prodrug: deacetylated to 6-monoacetylmorphine (6-MAM) then morphine in the CNS. 2–3× morphine potency (better CNS entry). Not legal in the US.

Hydromorphone (Dilaudid) / oxymorphone

Morphine analogs with a C6 ketone (oxymorphone also a C14-OH). Higher lipid solubility → faster action, 6–10× more potent than morphine. Hydromorphone is a go-to in renal impairment.

Hydrocodone / oxycodone

Codeine analogs (keep the 3-OCH₃): hydrocodone 6–8×, oxycodone 9–10× as potent as oral codeine (roughly morphine-strength orally). Intrinsic μ activity (not prodrugs); minor conversion to hydromorphone (<10%) and oxymorphone (~1%). Combined with acetaminophen (Vicodin, Percocet) or aspirin (Percodan); OxyContin = sustained-release oxycodone.

CheckWhy is heroin more potent than morphine if it is just converted to morphine?
The two acetyl groups make it lipophilic, so far more drug crosses the BBB quickly; it is then converted to 6-MAM and morphine inside the brain, trapping the active drug where it acts.

Q6. Fentanyl, methadone, tramadol: what makes each special?

Fentanyl fastest

  • Purely synthetic, extremely lipophilic (intense euphoria); ~100× morphine
  • Fastest peak analgesia of the group; rapid onset and termination: IV peak analgesia ~5 min (morphine ~15 min); ends by redistribution to fat
  • No active metabolites (CYP3A4 → inactive norfentanyl) → good in renal failure
  • Uses: anesthesia; spinal analgesia (little rostral spread → localized analgesia); transdermal patch for chronic (cancer) pain in opioid-tolerant patients (slow kinetics); buccal lollipop discontinued Oct 2024
  • Analogs: sufentanil (1,000×), alfentanil, carfentanil (10,000×, veterinary); illicit fentanyl now drives most overdose deaths

Methadone longest t½

  • Equipotent with morphine; side effects similar
  • Longest half-life of the group; very slow kinetics: t½ ~15–40 h (accumulates over days → titrate slowly)
  • Prolongs QT → torsades (ECG monitoring)
  • D-isomer blocks NMDA receptors → useful in chronic nociceptive + neuropathic pain; may blunt tolerance
  • Uses: detoxification and maintenance for opioid use disorder (daily observed dosing in a certified program), and chronic pain

Tramadol ceiling

  • Synthetic codeine analog; weak (partial) μ agonist + NE and 5-HT reuptake inhibitor → combined analgesia
  • Ceiling on analgesia, with limited respiratory depression and abuse potential
  • Good for mild–moderate pain (step 2 of the analgesic ladder)
  • Toxicity: lowers seizure threshold; serotonin syndrome with SSRIs/MAOIs; active metabolite needs CYP2D6
CheckA patient is switched from morphine to an equianalgesic dose of methadone. What changes?
Potency is similar, side effects are similar and no withdrawal occurs (both full agonists), but the dosing interval changes because methadone's half-life is much longer. Also watch the QT.
Step 1 HY — methadone and the heart

Syncope or palpitations in a patient on methadone maintenance → QT prolongation → torsades de pointes. Interactions via CYP3A4 (inducers like rifampin can precipitate withdrawal; inhibitors raise levels).

Q7. Meperidine, loperamide and the other "special purpose" opioids

Meperidine (Demerol)

Not a preferred analgesic. Metabolite normeperidine is toxic → CNS excitation, tremor, seizures (accumulates in renal failure and with repeated dosing). Has serotonergic activity → serotonin syndrome with MAOIs (also SSRIs). Anticholinergic → may cause mydriasis rather than miosis. Short-term niche: post-anesthesia shivering.

Loperamide (Imodium)

Piperidine μ agonist that poorly enters the CNS (P-gp efflux) → no euphoria or analgesia but full constipating effect → OTC antidiarrheal. Massive doses (abuse) → QT prolongation, torsades.

Diphenoxylate (Lomotil)

GI-selective opioid for diarrhea; combined with atropine to deter abuse.

Dextromethorphan

Antitussive (cough center) at doses that give no analgesia; also an NMDA antagonist (dissociative "robo-tripping" at high dose) and serotonergic → serotonin syndrome with MAOIs/SSRIs.

Q5. How do abuse-deterrent formulations work?

They exploit route-dependent bioavailability: the antagonist is harmless when the pill is taken as intended but becomes active if the tablet is crushed and injected or snorted.

Naloxone included in the tablet

  • Naloxone is inactive orally/sublingually (first pass) but fully active if injected
  • Oxycodone + naloxone (2:1) (Targin)
  • Buprenorphine + naloxone (4:1) (Suboxone, Zubsolv)

Naltrexone sequestered in the tablet

  • Naltrexone core stays locked inside and passes through unabsorbed if swallowed whole
  • Released only if the tablet is crushed → blocks the high (and precipitates withdrawal)
  • Morphine ER + naltrexone (Embeda)
CheckWhy does Suboxone contain naloxone, if naloxone does nothing when taken sublingually?
That is the point: sublingual = naloxone inactive, buprenorphine works. If someone dissolves and injects it, naloxone becomes active, blunting the effect and precipitating withdrawal.
Memory tool

"Swallow = safe, Shoot = sick." Ratios: Oxy 2 : 1, Bupe 4 : 1 (Suboxone = "Sub-four").

11A 66-year-old man with severe COPD (FEV₁ 35% predicted, baseline PaCO₂ 50 mm Hg) has chronic back pain not controlled by acetaminophen, NSAIDs or duloxetine. A long-acting opioid is planned. Which drug's property makes it the safest choice for him?

A. Buprenorphine is a partial μ agonist with a ceiling on respiratory depression, a key advantage with limited respiratory reserve. B: NMDA block helps neuropathic pain, not breathing; methadone accumulates. C: low bioavailability is not a safety feature. D: no ceiling, and patches are for opioid-tolerant patients. E: κ agonism causes dysphoria/psychotomimesis.

12Researchers create a mouse in which μ-opioid receptors are deleted only from GABAergic interneurons of the ventral tegmental area. Compared with wild-type mice, which response to morphine would be most reduced?

D. Opioids produce reward by inhibiting VTA GABA interneurons, disinhibiting dopamine neurons that project to the nucleus accumbens. Without μ on those interneurons, reward is blunted. A: Edinger-Westphal. B: enteric neurons. C: medullary respiratory centers. E: dorsal horn.

13A 25-year-old with travelers' diarrhea buys an over-the-counter μ-opioid agonist. It slows his bowel movements but causes no euphoria, sedation or analgesia at recommended doses. Which property accounts for the absence of central effects?

C. Loperamide has limited CNS entry because P-gp efflux keeps it out of the brain; its μ agonism acts on enteric neurons. B describes diphenoxylate (Lomotil, prescription), where atropine deters abuse rather than preventing CNS entry. A, D, E are incorrect.

14A 34-year-old woman completed a 10-day inpatient opioid detoxification and has had a negative urine drug screen for 8 days. She does not want an opioid agonist and asks for a once-daily pill that will block the effect of any opioid she might use. Which drug is most appropriate?

B. Naltrexone is an oral, long-acting (~24 h), high-affinity μ antagonist used to prevent relapse; she is opioid-free long enough to avoid precipitated withdrawal. A: inactive orally and short-acting. C: does not enter the CNS. D and E are agonist therapies she declined.
